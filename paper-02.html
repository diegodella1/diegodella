The Case for Message-Market Fit as a Completion of PMF

Diego Dell Agostino
Section I  /  Five

The Incomplete Victory of Product-Market Fit

Product-Market Fit has become the organizing principle of modern product development. Find the right problem, build the right solution, and growth follows. The framework is sound. The logic is clean. And for a generation of builders, it has served as the clearest signal that a product is worth scaling.

PMF is necessary. It is not sufficient. The market can need something and still not recognize it when it arrives.

But there is a category of failure that PMF does not account for; one that I have seen repeat itself across industries and company sizes. A product achieves genuine fit with a real market need. The retention numbers are there. The problem is real. The solution works. And yet the product stalls. Acquisition gets expensive, adoption slows, and at some point someone in the room proposes doubling the marketing budget to solve a problem that was never a marketing problem to begin with.

PMF tells you the product belongs in the market. It does not tell you whether the market can recognize it when it arrives.

Section II  /  Five

The Organizational Gap

The reason this failure mode persists is structural. In most organizations, the people responsible for building the product and the people responsible for describing it operate under different incentives, different timelines, and different definitions of success. The product team measures fit through behavior (retention, engagement, usage patterns). The communications team measures fit through perception (awareness, sentiment, conversion). These are not the same thing, and the gap between them is where coherence breaks down.

A product can solve the right problem and still be described in a way that attracts the wrong user. It can have genuine depth and still be positioned in a way that signals the wrong value. This is not a failure of either team; it is a failure of the system that keeps them separate. When the people who understand the product most deeply are not the same people shaping how it is introduced to the world, the result is almost always a story that undersells, misdirects, or simply fails to land.

The market does not experience a product in isolation. It experiences the narrative first, the product second; and that sequence is not neutral. When the two experiences confirm each other, trust forms quickly and acquisition becomes cheaper over time. When they don't, the user feels a gap they may not be able to articulate but will absolutely act on. They leave, or they stay confused, or they never convert in the first place. In each case, the cost is real and the cause is invisible to anyone looking only at the product metrics.

Section III  /  Five

Message-Market Fit

What I am proposing is not a replacement for PMF. It is the dimension that completes it.

The Completion Model
PMF
Product belongs in market
+ MMF
Recognition
Market can identify the product

Message-Market Fit is the state in which the way a product is described produces the same recognition as the product itself. It is achieved when the user's first encounter with the narrative (a manifesto, a landing page, a pitch) creates an expectation that the product then confirms exactly. Not approximately. Exactly.

Think of the products you have seen grow without apparent effort. Not because they outspent the competition, but because every person who encountered them seemed to already understand what they were for. That is not luck and it is not virality. It is the result of a story and a product that have been aligned so precisely that the user's recognition does the work that advertising usually has to buy.

MMF is not a messaging exercise. It is a coherence test. If the story and the product are not confirming each other at every touchpoint, the system is leaking value somewhere. The question is where.

Section IV  /  Five

Operating Narratives

The practical mechanism for achieving MMF is what I call an Operating Narrative. This is not a positioning document or a brand brief. It is a foundational text that defines, before anything is built or launched, the claim the product is making about the world and the specific user it is making that claim to.

An Operating Narrative answers three questions that a PRD does not. What is the worldview this product assumes the user already holds? What is the single recognition moment we are engineering toward? And what does the product have to be true, technically, for the narrative to hold?

The Operating Narrative is where strategy becomes engineering. Where "we believe" becomes "therefore we build."

That last question is the most important. It is the point where narrative becomes an engineering constraint rather than a marketing afterthought. When the Operating Narrative is clear, every subsequent decision (feature prioritization, onboarding design, even pricing) has a test it can be run against. The question is no longer whether something moves a metric. The question is whether it makes the product more or less true to what it claimed to be.

Section V  /  Five

Toward a More Coherent Organization

Here is the pattern I have observed: companies that achieve PMF without MMF tend to grow through force. More spend, more sales headcount, more content. The machine works but it requires constant fuel. The moment the fuel slows, so does the growth.

Companies that achieve both tend to grow through recognition. The right user encounters the product and feels, almost immediately, that it was built for them. That feeling is not an accident. It is the result of a system in which the story and the product have been aligned from the beginning; not reconciled after the fact.

✦   ❧   ✦

PMF without MMF is a product that works and has to be explained. MMF without PMF is a promise the product cannot keep.

The work is to close that gap before it costs you the users who were always yours to lose.
