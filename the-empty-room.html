The EmptyRoom

On engagement as a measure of human presence, and what it measures when most of the presence is automated.

Diego Dell'Agostino  ·  Buenos Aires

The numbers were fine. That was the first problem.

You published something last week, or last month. The impressions came in. Engagement rate looked fine. Everything the dashboard uses to tell you the thing worked was telling you the thing worked. But something was off. Not dramatically, not silence, just the echo coming back slightly wrong. Responses that felt produced rather than felt. Shares without weight behind them. Like the content passed through people without leaving a mark.

You probably filed it under off week. Algorithm change. Audience fatigue. There's always an explanation available when you need one.

But the feeling came back. And then again.

Section 01 The Instrument

Engagement metrics were always measuring behavior. Not presence, not attention, not a human on the other side of the screen who actually read the thing. Behavior. The click. The scroll-stop. The repost. A signal that something happened, with no information about who made it happen.

For twenty years that didn't matter much, because behavior mostly implied a human. We knew it wasn't perfect: click fraud existed, click farms existed, whole industries grew up around detecting and filtering the noise. But the assumption held well enough to build on. Non-human behavior was the exception you corrected for. The signal, underneath the fraud, still pointed to a person.

That's what changed. Agents aren't fraud. They're legitimate traffic. They don't show up as anomalies in your analytics because they aren't anomalies. The system has no reason to filter them out and, increasingly, no way to tell them apart.

The room didn't empty overnight. It filled, with something that behaves exactly like the audience used to behave.

Agents are browsing. Agents are transacting. The web already has llms.txt, a proposed standard modeled on robots.txt, that tells AI crawlers how to read your site. When the infrastructure starts building protocols specifically for non-human visitors, that's not a warning sign. That's a receipt. It already happened, while you were looking at your analytics and feeling vaguely unsettled.

The dashboard still glows. The echo is just slightly wrong.

Section 02 What It Proves

The problem isn't that the audience changed. Audiences change all the time, that's the whole history of media. The problem is that the instrument we use to measure the audience was never measuring what we thought. It was measuring behavior. And behavior doesn't require a person anymore.

So what does any of it prove? A high-engagement post proves the algorithm rewarded it. A high-traffic article proves something requested it. Neither tells you whether a human read the thing, cared about it, or will think about it tomorrow.

The content is still being produced. The metrics are still being generated. The feedback loop is still spinning. It's just that somewhere in that loop, the human became optional.

The dashboard still glows the same color it always did.

Section 03 The Real Cost

This isn't an argument against publishing, and it's not a warning about what's coming. The thing that bothers me is simpler: the metrics we use to evaluate whether we're reaching people were never designed to distinguish between a person and a process that mimics one.

They still aren't.

Which means every decision downstream of those metrics (what to publish, when, for whom, in what register) is being made on the basis of a signal that has always been a proxy, and is now a proxy for something increasingly different from what we intended. The optimization loop is intact. It's just that nobody checked what it's optimizing toward.

Think about what that means in practice. You A/B test a headline. The variant with more clicks wins. You write more headlines like it. The system learns. You learn. Both of you get better at producing something that generates behavior. But if the behavior is increasingly generated by agents reading for indexing, summarizing for retrieval, or scanning for relevance signals on behalf of a user who will never see your page at all, then you've spent six months getting very good at writing for a reader who isn't there. The reader who isn't there still generates behavior.

The room isn't empty. That's the thing. It's full. It's just not full of who you think.

Section 04 The Question This Reading Path Will Carry

I'm not going to answer the question I actually want to answer here. That one takes the rest of this series. But I want to leave it somewhere it can sit:

If engagement measures behavior, and agents can produce behavior, is there anything in the relationship between a creator and an audience that an agent fundamentally cannot simulate?

Not at scale. Not statistically. Not at all.

I think yes. I think there's something in that relationship that doesn't survive delegation. But we've never had to name it, because we've never had to defend it. We just called it "connection" and assumed the metrics were picking it up.

They weren't. They were tracking clicks.

The clicks are still coming. The question is who's making them.
