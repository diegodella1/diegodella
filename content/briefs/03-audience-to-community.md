# Editorial brief 03

## Working title

From Audience to Community: The Product Layer Media Still Gets Wrong

## Core question

What separates an audience from a community, and why do media products repeatedly confuse reach or engagement with durable participation?

## Thesis

An audience receives; a community can recognize itself, participate, form relationships, and carry shared language beyond the publisher's surfaces. Content can attract and retain attention, but community requires product choices around identity, rituals, feedback, contribution, governance, and ownership. Treating every engaged audience as a community produces weak products and false expectations.

## Why this matters

Automated production and distribution make content supply abundant. Durable relationships become more valuable precisely because output and reach are easier to manufacture. Media organizations need a sharper model for deciding whether they are building an audience, serving users, or enabling a community—and what each one requires.

## Audience

Media founders, audience development and marketing teams, community leads, product managers, creators, membership businesses, and editorial leaders.

## Key arguments

1. Audience, users, and community describe different relationships rather than stages in a guaranteed funnel.
2. Reach and repeat consumption can exist without member-to-member recognition or participation.
3. Community is partly a product and governance problem: identity, roles, rituals, contribution paths, moderation, and exit matter.
4. Content supplies shared objects and language, but publishing frequency alone does not create belonging.
5. Marketing can acquire attention; product design determines what people can do with it after arrival.
6. Owned audience channels matter, but ownership of a list is not ownership of a relationship.
7. Automation can support onboarding, routing, summarization, and moderation while still failing to create trust.
8. The useful strategic question is not “How do we build community?” but “What relationship are people asking this product to support?”

## Practical examples

- Use Posta to discuss the documented interaction between programming, packaging, listening behavior, distribution, audience feedback, and repeat listening. Verify any specific community mechanism before publication.
- Compare a show subscriber, a registered product user, and an active community member to expose different rights, behaviors, and expectations.
- Use Referí and LiveOverlay as public product examples of participation surfaces; do not claim they constitute communities by themselves.
- Use DecisionOS to discuss audience research as an input to product decisions, while distinguishing synthetic research from a living community.
- Use the Constitutive Arc to explore how shared language can travel through an audience without assuming that transformation automatically creates community.

## Counterarguments

- Some media products need only a high-quality audience relationship and should not build a community layer.
- The boundary between audience and community is porous; lightweight participation can still be valuable.
- Community rhetoric can conceal unpaid labor, extraction, or governance failures.
- Platforms may supply useful community infrastructure even when the publisher does not own the channel.
