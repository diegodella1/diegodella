<div class="content">
<div class="essay-section intro reveal-stagger">
<p class="reveal">Last November, my son came home from school with a disposable camera. A friend had given it to him. Not a phone case, not a gift card. A yellow Kodak with 27 exposures and no screen.</p>
<div class="article-opening-diagram"><Block0 /></div>
<p class="reveal">He'd seen them on TikTok. He'd never held one. He kept turning it around looking for the preview button. "Where's the photo?" he asked. You don't see it, I said. You take it and you wait. He stared at me like I'd just described a telegram.</p>
<p class="reveal">Then he shot 27 frames in an hour. He was more careful with each one than he'd ever been with anything on a screen. He aimed. He framed. He wasted three on the cat and felt the loss physically. When we got the prints back, he held them like artifacts from another civilization. A picture of a tree. A blurry shot of his own sneakers. The cat, mid-yawn, overexposed.</p>
<p class="reveal">He pinned them to his bedroom wall. They're still there.</p>
<p class="reveal">I see a similar desire for limits in some of the changes discussed here, from the things people buy to the communities they choose. I want to examine the connection without assuming that a child’s camera and a country’s trade policy have the same cause.</p>

</div>

<div class="essay-section reveal-stagger" id="tgc-s1">
<div class="section-header reveal">
<div class="section-header-num">I</div>
<div class="section-header-body">
<div class="section-header-label">The Premise</div>
<div class="section-header-title">Openness as dogma</div>
</div>
</div>
<p class="reveal">The last thirty years had one commandment: open. Open your borders. Open your markets. Open your mind. Open your identity. Open your source code. Globalization was progress. Disruption was virtue. If you didn't want to open up, something was wrong with you. You were old. You were afraid. You deserved to be left behind.</p>
<p class="reveal">It wasn't all wrong. The internet did connect the world. A kid in Lagos could suddenly watch the same lecture as a kid in Boston. That part was real. But openness was never presented as one option among many. <strong>It was presented as the only option. A complete ideology disguised as weather.</strong> You don't argue with weather. You adapt or you're out.</p>
<p class="reveal">Meanwhile, that kid in Lagos. He's not drowning in algorithmic overload. He's trying to get online. And the developed world already tried to help him: remember Negroponte's One Laptop Per Child? A hundred-dollar green laptop was going to save the developing world through connectivity. It was the dogma of openness applied to development policy: connect everyone and everything improves. It didn't work. The laptops broke, the infrastructure didn't exist, and the assumption that access equals progress turned out to be the same fallacy dressed in humanitarian clothing. What I'm about to describe is mostly a story about what happens to people who got everything the expansion promised and still ended up empty. But don't mistake that for a complete picture. Half the planet got the dogma without the delivery.</p>
<p class="reveal">Beneath all that expansion, something was eroding. The ability of people, communities, and entire countries to decide their own shape. To say: this is what we are, this is what we're not, and here's where our edges are. Edges became a dirty word. Boundaries meant you had something to hide. So everything opened. Everything got exposed. And nobody asked what happens when you remove all the walls from a building.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section reveal-stagger" id="tgc-s2">
<div class="section-header reveal">
<div class="section-header-num">II</div>
<div class="section-header-body">
<div class="section-header-label">The Toll</div>
<div class="section-header-title">What exposure costs</div>
</div>
</div>
<p class="reveal">A building needs some boundaries to remain usable. I find that image helpful when thinking about exposure, although it does not prove how a society should organize itself.</p>
<p class="reveal">We built supply chains that cross twenty countries to manufacture a single chip, and then acted surprised when some of those countries turned out to be adversaries. We offshored entire industries because labor was cheaper somewhere else, and left behind communities with no Plan B and a lot of justified anger. We called it efficiency. It was fragility with good marketing.</p>
<p class="reveal">We made transgression mandatory. You had to push a boundary to be relevant. Break something. Challenge something. For a while, that produced genuinely interesting things. Then it ate itself. When everyone is breaking the rules, nobody is breaking anything. Transgression became the most efficient form of conformism. The most predictable thing you could do in 2015 was be edgy.</p>
<p class="reveal">An old sentence can reach a new audience without the context in which it was said. Sometimes that brings overdue scrutiny. Sometimes the judgment exceeds what the available record supports. I worry about systems that reward a fast verdict while making correction much harder to circulate.</p>
<div class="pull-quote reveal">
<p>The problem with total accountability is that it applies infinite punishment to finite mistakes.</p>
</div>
<p class="reveal">That possibility can change how people speak. I have seen public language become more careful without the underlying disagreement becoming easier to discuss. The question is whether a community can hold people accountable while leaving room for context, correction, and proportion.</p>
<p class="reveal">The endless feed is the part I recognize in my own day: no clear stopping point, and a new reason to keep looking after the reason I opened it has passed. That experience motivates this essay. It does not tell me how everyone else feels about digital life.</p>
<div class="source-ref reveal">Personal observation</div>
</div>
</div>

<div class="interlude">
<div class="interlude-text reveal"> History doesn't <span class="stroke">repeat.</span><br />But it <span class="accent">rhymes.</span> </div>
</div>

<div class="content">
<div class="essay-section reveal-stagger" id="tgc-s3">
<div class="section-header reveal">
<div class="section-header-num">III</div>
<div class="section-header-body">
<div class="section-header-label">The Pattern</div>
<div class="section-header-title">The rhyme, not the repetition</div>
</div>
</div>

<p class="reveal">Periods of expansion can be followed by attempts to rebuild familiar forms of stability. I use that historical comparison cautiously: old materials can serve new purposes, and a resemblance between two periods does not establish a cycle that every society must follow.</p>
<p class="reveal">That's The Great Contraction. Not a retreat. A recombination. We are building post-algorithmic lives using pre-algorithmic tools. The vinyl a 22-year-old buys today is not his grandfather's vinyl. His grandfather didn't choose it against a backdrop of infinite streaming. His grandfather played a record because there was no other option. The kid plays a record because it's a deliberate act of refusal.</p>
<div class="pull-quote reveal">
<p>Same object. Different architecture. That's the rhyme.</p>
</div>
<p class="reveal">I group these examples under The Great Contraction because they involve attempts to reduce exposure and recover some control. The causes and consequences differ. A tariff is a policy with effects on other people; buying a simpler phone is a personal choice. The comparison is a question to investigate, not evidence of one movement behind both.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="essay-section reveal-stagger" id="tgc-s4">
<div class="section-header reveal">
<div class="section-header-num">IV</div>
<div class="section-header-body">
<div class="section-header-label">The Shadow</div>
<div class="section-header-title">The density reflex (and its shadow)</div>
</div>
</div>
<p class="reveal">The old money aesthetic is not aspirational wealth. It's aspirational permanence. A kid doesn't buy a cashmere sweater because he wants to look rich. He buys it because he wants one thing in his life that won't be irrelevant in three weeks. Vinyl records aren't nostalgia. They are objects that refuse to disappear. You can't delete a vinyl. You can't algorithmically deprecate it. It just sits there, being real, in a world where almost nothing else is.</p>
<p class="reveal">The return to God, country, family works the same way. These are not political positions. They are structures dense enough to resist the current. Faith, land, blood ties: you can disagree with all of them, but you can't deny they have weight. They are among the few things left that can't be erased by a software update.</p>
<p class="reveal">The same structures that offer shelter can also exclude people.</p>
<p class="reveal">Those same structures of shelter have been, historically, structures of exclusion. The family that "closes ranks" can be a refuge, or it can be a cage for whoever doesn't fit the mold. The nation that "protects its own" can be sovereign, or it can be a machine for deciding who counts as "its own" and who doesn't.</p>
<div class="pull-quote reveal">
<p>Every dense structure has a door. And every door has someone on the wrong side of it.</p>
</div>
<p class="reveal">Choosing to read a book does not explain a country’s exclusionary policies. What connects these examples for me is the question of boundaries: who gets to set them, who benefits, and who bears the cost? The consequences depend on those decisions.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section reveal-stagger" id="tgc-s5">
<div class="section-header reveal">
<div class="section-header-num">V</div>
<div class="section-header-body">
<div class="section-header-label">The Bifurcation</div>
<div class="section-header-title">FIFA or nobody</div>
</div>
</div>
<p class="reveal">For most of the 20th century, culture had a center. Everyone watched the same show. Heard the same song. Argued about it at the coffee machine. There was a commons. A shared arena where millions of strangers processed the same stories at the same time.</p>
<p>Large shared events and small communities serve different reasons to gather. I see pressure on the media businesses between them, but their prospects depend on their audience and costs.</p>
<div class="pull-quote reveal">

</div>
<p class="reveal">Sports offer examples of organizations seeking larger shared audiences, while smaller creators build around particular interests. I see pressure at both ends. That observation does not establish that a media business between them has no viable future. The audience, costs, and reason to return still matter.</p>
<p class="reveal">And the rhyme: these manufactured mega-events look exactly like the old broadcast commons. Billions watching at the same time. Same shape. But the old commons was a byproduct of limited channels. Three networks, one radio dial. Everyone watched the same thing because there was nothing else on. The new mega-commons is engineered at enormous cost: Netflix documentaries, Instagram reels, Saudi money, streaming deals. Deliberately manufactured to punch through infinite noise. Same shape. Different physics.</p>
<p class="reveal">Music tells the same story. Streaming gave everyone access to everything, and the result is that the number of songs reaching mass audiences keeps declining even as total streams rise. YouTube generated over $60 billion in revenue in 2025. Meanwhile, underground producers on SoundCloud are building scenes around raw, imperfect, human taste. They'd rather have a small room that feels real than a giant feed that feels like nothing.</p>
<div class="source-ref reveal">BBC, "YouTube Revenues," 2025 · MIDiA Research, "Anti-Algorithm Counter-Culture," 2024</div>
<p class="reveal">The business models that produce culture are contracting too. Netflix cut content spend. Streaming platforms are consolidating. The era of burning infinite cash to acquire infinite subscribers is over. Media companies are doing what countries are doing: reducing exposure, protecting margins, pulling back. The Great Contraction isn't just cultural. It's industrial.</p>
<p class="reveal">And when culture no longer gives you a shared language, you retreat to whatever does. Family references. Local slang. Religious texts. Vintage objects. The old money aesthetic works because it points to a code that predates algorithms. It says: this was here before the feed, and it'll be here after it collapses. The things that survive are the things that don't need the platform to exist.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section reveal-stagger" id="tgc-s6">
<div class="section-header reveal">
<div class="section-header-num">VI</div>
<div class="section-header-body">
<div class="section-header-label">The Contradiction</div>
<div class="section-header-title">The disposable that wants to last</div>
</div>
</div>
<p class="reveal">Here's where this gets uncomfortable. For everyone. Including me.</p>
<p class="reveal">The music figures complicate a simple story of retreat. In the <a href="https://www.riaa.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/RIAA-2024Year-End-Revenue-Report.pdf">RIAA’s 2024 U.S. report</a>, vinyl revenue grew by about 7%, while units grew by about 1%. Streaming still accounted for 84% of recorded music revenue. Growth in one physical format does not show that people are broadly abandoning digital consumption.</p>
<p class="reveal">Half of us say we want out. And the machine is winning anyway. The contraction is real as desire. It's failing as behavior. We want to close. We can't stop opening. That's The Great Contraction in its purest form. Not a success story. A tension.</p>
<p class="reveal">The appeal of stability also deserves care. A secondhand sweater or a record may be a practical purchase, a matter of taste, or a way to imagine a more settled life. I cannot infer someone’s housing, pension, or motives from what they browse. My interpretation needs to remain a question about the attraction, not a biography assigned to a generation.</p>
<div class="pull-quote reveal">

</div>
<p>The same feed can sell you a desire for permanence and a new aesthetic next week. That tension interests me more than deciding whether everyone buying the sweater shares one motive.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section reveal-stagger" id="tgc-s7">
<div class="section-header reveal">
<div class="section-header-num">VII</div>
<div class="section-header-body">
<div class="section-header-label">The Reckoning</div>
<div class="section-header-title">The part that costs me</div>
</div>
</div>
<p class="reveal">I'm 43. I have a son. I grew up in Buenos Aires copying cassettes from the radio and I now make a living building broadcast tools that run on AI. I am part of the generation that walked across the bridge from analog to digital, and I can tell you: neither side was paradise.</p>
<p class="reveal">I should also tell you that I spent the last three years accelerating the exact thing I'm now writing against. I build tools that automate content. I ship products that compress human labor into algorithmic output. I am not writing this from outside the machine. I'm writing it from the cockpit. And that's the part that costs me: I can see the contraction clearly because I helped build what made it necessary.</p>
<p class="reveal">I catch myself romanticizing the contraction. Of course I do. I remember what it was like to have a phone that was just a phone. I remember what it was like to be bored. I remember what it was like to sit with a thought for more than twelve seconds before the next one was algorithmically injected into my skull. And I want that back. I want it for my kid.</p>
<p class="reveal">But I also remember what the closed world was. I remember Argentina in the 90s. I remember what protectionism looked like when it wasn't a think piece but an actual policy that made everything expensive and kept the wrong people in power. Contraction can be shelter. It can also be suffocation. Sovereignty can mean autonomy, or it can mean isolation. The same density that feels like roots can be the thing that strangles you.</p>
<p class="reveal">And here's the biggest risk. Not that the world contracts. Contracting might be exactly what's needed. The risk is that contraction becomes the next dogma. That "closing" becomes the only permitted gesture, just as "opening" was. That permanence gets fetishized the way disruption was. That someone builds a political movement, a brand, an identity around the idea that inwardness is always right.</p>
<p class="reveal">Because that's exactly what happened with openness. And it was wrong then, too.</p>
<div class="pull-quote reveal">
<p>The problem was never the direction. The problem was always the dogma.</p>
</div>
<p class="reveal">Opening everything was a dogma. Closing everything will be another. What we actually need isn't a direction. It's the capacity to choose one, and to change it when it stops working, and to not destroy anyone who disagrees. Structures that can be dense and open at the same time. Things with edges that aren't walls. Things with roots that aren't cages.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section reveal-stagger">
<p class="reveal">My son still has those photos on his wall. The blurry ones. The overexposed cat. He hasn't asked for another disposable camera. He went right back to the iPad.</p>
<p class="reveal">But he kept the photos. He chose what stays.</p>
