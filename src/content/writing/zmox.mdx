<h2 class="section-title fade-in">The Collapse</h2>
<div class="section-rule fade-in"></div>
<p class="dropcap fade-in"> Around 1830–32, Katsushika Hokusai designed <a href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/56353">Under the Wave off Kanagawa</a>, a woodblock print. </p>
<div class="article-opening-diagram"><Block0 /></div>
<p>The wave hangs over the boats. Behind it, Mount Fuji is small and still. I keep returning to the contrast between the immediate danger and the recurring subject in the distance.</p>


<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> Every media organization operating today is inside the wave. The fragmentation of attention, the collapse of direct traffic, algorithmic distribution, generative AI: real, immediate, violent. The rowers are right to hold on. What they cannot afford is to mistake the wave for the structure of the world. </p>
<p class="fade-in">Hokusai’s Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji shows a recurring subject from different places and under different conditions. I use that series as an image for a media source with a recognizable perspective across many encounters. That is my reading of the work, not a claim about Hokusai’s design intentions.</p>

<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p>Reach, attention, trust, and revenue measure different parts of a media business. I want to add a question those measures do not answer on their own: what does a reader learn through repeated contact with a source?</p>

<p>Traffic tells a team which work traveled. It does not establish whether readers can explain the subject better, challenge an argument, or make an independent judgment afterward.</p>
<p class="fade-in"> Lippmann saw it in 1922: the pictures in our heads are not formed from direct experience. They come from the narratives we consume, repeatedly, over time. McLuhan pushed further: the medium itself shapes cognitive structure, independent of content. What both were circling is this: <strong>the primary function of a media organization is not to distribute information. It is to participate in how its audience constructs reality.</strong> </p>

<p class="fade-in page-inline-ccaa3e45c9">I use “constitutive” to ask whether contact with a source contributes to how a reader understands a domain. That is a stronger claim than increasing time spent. It needs evidence of learning and independent judgment; the existence of a coherent publication does not establish it.</p>

<figure class="diagram fade-in" aria-label="Dalio's Three Pillars of dominant order"> <Block1 /> </figure>
<div class="pull fade-in"> The medium that loses narrative authority does not merely lose audience share. It loses its constitutive function: the ability to be part of how people build their understanding of reality. </div>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> Ray Dalio maps how dominant orders rise and fall. The scale is empires and currencies, but the mechanism transfers. Dominant orders don't fall from frontal attack. They fall when an emergent force creates new terrain where the old order's advantages are irrelevant, captures the distribution layer, and by the time the incumbent notices, the perimeter has already moved. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Three pillars hold a dominant order. Control of productive resources. Institutional legitimacy (the social agreement that this order is the right one). And narrative superiority: the capacity to define how reality gets interpreted. When all three align, the order is self-reinforcing. When two crack, the third cannot hold alone. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Pre-digital media held all three. The infrastructure was capital-intensive, physically scarce, legally licensed. Owning a printing press or a broadcast license was not a content advantage; it was a structural barrier that made content advantages permanent. Legitimacy was codified: the press as the fourth estate, the byline as a credential. Narrative superiority was the automatic consequence of the other two. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The platforms never attacked this directly. They had no interest in journalism. They built adjacent infrastructure (social connection, search, video) and in doing so quietly took the first pillar. Distribution. The trade routes. Once the routes changed hands, legitimacy began to shake, because legitimacy without reach is philosophy, not power. And when legitimacy shook, narrative authority drifted. </p>
<p class="fade-in">When it drifted, it went somewhere. Authority is not destroyed. It migrates.</p>

<figure class="diagram fade-in" aria-label="Narrative authority migration timeline"> <Block2 /> </figure>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> The Facebook feed of 2012 was not a neutral surface. It had editorial logic. It promoted content that generated emotional reaction. It suppressed content that generated reflection. Over hundreds of millions of daily interactions, it was building something: a version of reality organized around outrage, affiliation, and the compulsive need to know what happened next. Not by intention. By optimization. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> TikTok's For You Page is more precise and more consequential. It doesn't recommend content. It learns, at the individual level, the exact emotional frequencies that keep a specific person watching. It constructs for each user a personalized narrative environment calibrated to their particular vulnerabilities, curiosities, and desires. It is, in the most literal sense, a transformation engine. Not because anyone designed the transformation. Because that is what you get when you optimize for watch time at scale. </p>
<p class="fade-in">The reader may not have chosen the sequence explicitly.</p>
<p class="fade-in"> The mechanism is visible now. When Facebook restructured its News Feed algorithm in 2012 to prioritize social engagement over chronological publishing, every publisher with distribution dependency on the platform lost a portion of narrative authority in a single policy change, with no input, no recourse. The authority migrated to the algorithm. The algorithm's editorial values became the de facto editorial values of every publisher whose reach ran through the feed. Publishers who recognized this built alternative distribution. Publishers who didn't found themselves a decade later producing content that satisfied the algorithm's values, because the algorithm was the only path to reach. The transformation of their audience was being designed daily by a system whose objectives were never disclosed. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The TikTok FYP completed what the Facebook feed began. Where Facebook at least nominally connected users to sources they'd chosen to follow, the FYP eliminated the source relationship entirely. The user doesn't follow The Atlantic or The Guardian or The Economist. The user follows the FYP, which decides what those publications mean to that user: which pieces they see, in what sequence, next to what other content. The lens through which any piece of journalism reaches a reader is not the publication's lens. It's the FYP's lens. The constitutive function is captured upstream before the reader even arrives. </p>
<div class="pull fade-in"> ZMOT explained how brands win before purchase. ZMOI explained how media brands win before belief. The Constitutive Arc asks the question that follows: what happens to the audience after belief, and who designed that transformation? </div>
<p class="fade-in page-inline-ccaa3e45c9"> On the genealogy: ZMOT was built on behavioral data from thousands of buyers: empirical, measurable, falsifiable. ZMOI and the Constitutive Arc are prescriptive (built on argument, not measurement). The genealogy is conceptual, not evidential. That distinction should be declared rather than obscured. What follows is a claim about how media organizations should be built, not a report on how they behave. </p>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in">Generative AI does not add a new problem. It reveals the depth of the existing one.</p>
<p class="fade-in"> In the broadcast era, the transformation arc (the sequence of information, interpretation, and meaning a person consumed over time) was designed by editors. Someone had a point of view. The author existed. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> In the algorithmic era, the arc was generated by optimization functions. The author became a system. Transformation still occurred, but as byproduct rather than intention. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> In the AI era, something different happens: <strong>the arc emerges from the interaction itself.</strong> The user is not consuming a narrative someone else made. They are participating in the real-time generation of a narrative that has never existed before. Every AI conversation, every hyperpersonalized feed, every recommendation engine that has learned the specific shape of your cognition: these are not distribution systems. They are co-authorship systems. They produce transformation arcs that are, in the strictest sense, unique to each individual. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Which means every media organization operating in an AI-mediated environment is producing personalized transformation arcs for its audience, whether it designed them or not. The arc exists. It occurs. The user experiences it and is changed. The only question is authorship. Did you design the transformation your system is producing? Or did you outsource it to a system optimizing for something else? </p>
<p class="fade-in"> If you haven't designed it, you haven't abdicated it. You've outsourced it. And the entity you outsourced it to has objectives that are not yours, and an audience relationship accountable to no values but its own. </p>
<div class="pull fade-in"> A media organization that does not intentionally design the transformation arc of its audience is not neutral. It is a passive conduit for transformations designed by other systems, toward other ends. </div>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in">Hokusai made thirty-six views of Fuji.</p>
<p>The series offers different views of the same mountain. For this essay, that is a useful image of variation around a recognizable subject.</p>

<p class="fade-in"> That is what a media organization can do at its highest. Not inform. Not entertain. Not even persuade. Give the audience a Fuji: something permanent, something that holds its shape across surfaces and crises. Show it from enough angles, with enough consistency, that the audience learns to see it themselves. Not to need you to see it. To have learned, through repeated encounter with your lens, a way of seeing that is now partly theirs. </p>
<p>The proposal is to build repeated encounters that help readers develop their own understanding. The sections below describe how a team might design and test them.</p>

<h2 class="section-title fade-in">The Field</h2>
<div class="section-rule fade-in"></div>
</div>
<p class="fade-in"> Before naming the architecture, we need to name what changed. Not the technology. Something more foundational: the shape of the relationship between a media organization and the person it is trying to reach. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> For most of mass media history, that relationship had a geometry. Linear. A source emitted. A receiver consumed. The message traveled in one direction, arrived more or less intact, and the receiver was changed in ways the source could anticipate and design for. The broadcast model was not just technical architecture. It was a theory of transformation: the author shapes the arc, the audience walks it. </p>
<p>People can enter through a single article, a clip, a recommendation, or a conversation. The order the publisher intended may have little to do with the order a reader encounters.</p>
<p class="fade-in"> What replaced it is a field. </p>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> In physics, a field is not an object. It is a region of space where every point has a value: a potential, a force, a direction. It doesn't push you along a predetermined path. It structures the conditions under which any path you choose will unfold. Walk north, south, east, or west through a gravitational field: the field is present everywhere, shaping the cost and consequence of every step, without dictating which step you take. </p>
<p class="fade-in page-inline-ccaa3e45c9"> The empirical anchor: Melanie Green and Timothy Brock's Narrative Transportation Theory (2000) demonstrated that deep engagement with a narrative produces measurable belief change through "transportation"—absorption into the story world that reduces counter-argumentation. Subsequent research confirmed these effects compound across repeated exposure and are more durable than attitude change from direct argument. The field model is a design framework for producing transportation conditions intentionally, across multiple surfaces, over time. I am using that research to propose a design model. The model still needs testing. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The organization no longer designs a path. It designs a field. Every user who enters walks their own version of it, shaped by their history, their attention, their entry point, their emotional state when they arrive. The path is unique. The field is shared. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Most media strategy hasn't absorbed this. It still designs content as if there's a sequence the audience follows. Measures success as if there's a single arc to optimize. Treats editorial voice as if consistent tone from the source produces consistent experience for the receiver. None of these assumptions survive the field. </p>
<div class="pull fade-in"> The organization no longer designs a path. It designs a field. Every user walks their own version of it. The path is unique. The field is shared. </div>

<figure class="diagram fade-in" aria-label="Path model vs Field model"> <Block3 /> </figure>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> What does it mean to design a field rather than a path? </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The unit of design is no longer the article, the episode, the newsletter, or the post. Those are artifacts. The unit of design is the <strong>condition of encounter</strong>: the set of forces present regardless of which artifact the user picks up, which surface they enter from, where in their own arc they happen to be. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> A well-designed field has three properties a well-designed path doesn't need, because in a path they're given by the sequence. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The first is <strong>coherence under variation</strong>. In a path, coherence is enforced by sequence. In a field, the user enters from any direction, at any point, in any order. Coherence can't be structural. It must be gravitational: something at the center of the field that pulls every encounter toward the same orientation, regardless of entry point. That center is the editorial lens. Not tone, not format, not platform. The lens: the specific, differentiated way the organization sees the world and makes that seeing contagious. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The second is <strong>directionality without determinism</strong>. The organization can't control which path the user takes, but it can design the field so that all paths share a direction, so that wherever users enter and however they move, they tend to emerge with more understanding than they arrived with. The field exerts a force. It doesn't prescribe a destination. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The third is <strong>transformation that compounds</strong>. In a path, transformation happens at the end. In a field, it's incremental and nonlinear. Each encounter adds a small charge. The charge accumulates across surfaces and time. What looks like a series of isolated contacts is a single extended process of constitution: the user becoming, gradually and through repetition, someone who sees the world through a lens they didn't have before. </p>
<div class="pull fade-in"> Coherence under variation. Directionality without determinism. Transformation that compounds. These are not content principles. They are field properties. They require a completely different kind of design. </div>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> The field model changes the answer to almost every strategic question. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Publishing cadence: in the path model, it's about maintaining momentum along a sequence. In the field model, it's about charge density: how frequently the user encounters the field, across how many surfaces, with what consistency of gravitational pull. Not how often you publish. How reliably the field is present in the user's day. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Platform strategy: in the path model, platforms distribute the same sequence. In the field model, each surface is a different angle of incidence into the same field. The Telegram message is the field encountered at close range, high trust, low commitment. The long-form analysis is the field at depth, with time, in a receptive state. The browser notification is the field arriving unannounced at the moment of maximum interpretive openness. Different surfaces. Same field. Different depths of the same well. </p>
<p class="fade-in">Completion tells you whether someone reached the end. CAX also asks whether their understanding changes over repeated encounters. That requires research with readers: what can they now explain, question, or investigate that they could not before? Retention and revenue still matter to the organization, but they do not substitute for evidence of learning.</p>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> There is an objection practitioners raise first, and it contains a real tension. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> If every user designs their own path through the field, the organization loses the ability to construct shared experience. And without shared experience, there is no community; only a large number of individual users having individual encounters with artifacts that happen to share a brand. </p>

<p class="fade-in"> Two people who walk different paths through the same gravitational field don't arrive at the same place. But they share something more durable than a common destination: a common orientation. They've been shaped by the same forces. They've developed, through their individual encounters with the field, a compatible way of seeing. That compatibility is the foundation of community—not identical content consumed simultaneously, but a shared lens through which different content is encountered and understood. </p>
<p>A shared perspective might connect readers who encounter different pieces at different times. That is a proposal about community, not evidence that asynchronous reading produces deeper understanding than a shared broadcast.</p>
<div class="pull fade-in"> The community the field produces is not one of simultaneous consumption. It is one of constituted identity: people who see the world compatibly because they were shaped by the same field, each in their own time, each through their own door. </div>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> The field model has a practitioner's question attached to it. This question is the diagnostic that separates organizations thinking in field terms from those that aren't. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Not: what is our content strategy? </p>
<p class="fade-in"> <strong>If a user encountered our field from ten different entry points, in ten different emotional states, on ten different surfaces, over six months—what would be the consistent force they felt pulling them in the same direction every time?</strong> </p>
<p class="fade-in"> If you can answer that with precision, you have a field. If the answer changes depending on the surface, the format, or who wrote the piece that day, you have a content operation. Content operations are not worthless. But they cannot produce the Constitutive Arc. Only fields can. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> A field is not a metaphor if it can be falsified. The following three proxies are the empirical floor: the minimum instrumentation required to distinguish a field from a content operation with aspirational language. </p>

<h2 class="section-title fade-in">The Ancestor</h2>
<div class="section-rule fade-in"></div>
</div>
<p>Campbell’s monomyth and Harmon’s story circle provide the narrative models I borrow here. They give me a way to plan changes in understanding, with limits that matter when applying a storytelling tool to readers.</p>
<p class="fade-in"> Joseph Campbell published <em>The Hero with a Thousand Faces</em> in 1949. His subject was mythology. His method was comparative: he read the sacred stories of hundreds of cultures, across centuries and continents, and found beneath their surface differences a single deep structure that repeated with uncanny consistency. He called it the monomyth. Others called it the Hero's Journey. The names are less important than what he found. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> What he found was this: across every culture that Campbell surveyed (and the selection has been disputed by folklorists and anthropologists who argue he chose examples to confirm a structure already in mind) the stories that survive tend to share the same underlying architecture. A person begins in a known world. Something calls them beyond its borders. They cross a threshold into unfamiliar territory. They encounter trials, allies, and enemies. They find something they could not have found without leaving. They bring it back. And they return changed, not to the same world they left, but to a world that looks the same and is fundamentally different because they are. </p>
<p class="fade-in page-inline-ccaa3e45c9"> Note on Campbell: The academic critique of his universalism is legitimate: he selected his examples, and the monomyth is abstract enough to map onto almost any narrative if applied loosely. The Constitutive Arc uses the monomyth not as anthropological fact but as an operational heuristic for thinking about transformation arcs. Its value here is practical, not ethnographic. If a better structural model for designed transformation exists, the framework should use that instead. </p>
<div class="pull fade-in"> I use Campbell’s monomyth here as a way to describe change over time. </div>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> Dan Harmon, the television writer, spent years trying to understand why certain stories work on audiences at a level that cannot be explained by craft alone. He arrived, independently, at Campbell. And then he did something Campbell had not done: he operationalized it. He compressed the monomyth into eight positions arranged in a circle, a tool that writers could use not just to analyze stories that already existed but to build new ones with the same deep structure. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The eight positions are: you are in a zone of comfort, you want something, you enter an unfamiliar situation, you adapt to it, you get what you wanted, you pay a heavy price for it, you return to your familiar situation, you have changed. The circle is not a sequence of scenes. It is a map of transformation. It describes not what happens in a story but what has to happen inside the protagonist for the story to feel true. </p>
<p>CAX borrows from Campbell and Harmon to ask how a sequence of encounters might support learning. That extension needs its own evidence; a useful model for fiction does not establish how every reader changes.</p>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>


<figure class="diagram fade-in" aria-label="Monomyth mapped to Constitutive Arc"> <Block5 /> </figure>
<p class="fade-in"> In the monomyth, the hero begins in the ordinary world: a place of comfort, familiarity, and incomplete understanding. In the Constitutive Arc, the ordinary world is the user before they encounter the field: existing frameworks inherited from prior sources, experience, ambient culture. Those frameworks are functional. They are also the starting condition: the state the field will gradually expand, complicate, and ultimately replace with something more sophisticated. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The call to adventure is the disruption that makes the ordinary world untenable. In the Constitutive Arc, the call is the first genuine contact with the field: the piece that reframes something the user thought they understood, the analysis that introduces a vocabulary they did not have, the argument that reveals a pattern they had been living inside without being able to name. The call is the moment the field makes itself felt as something other than ambient information. It exerts a pull. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The crossing of the threshold corresponds to the user's shift from casual contact to habitual engagement. They begin to return. They bring questions to the field rather than waiting for the field to raise questions for them. They are no longer passively receiving content. They are actively navigating the field, which means the field is beginning to shape not just what they know but how they think. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The road of trials is the extended middle: the accumulation of encounters across surfaces and time that gradually displace old frameworks with new ones. It is not dramatic. It does not announce itself. It is the slow, compound work of a field that is coherent enough, present enough, and differentiated enough to keep exerting its gravitational pull through months of ordinary contact. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The innermost cave (where the hero confronts the thing they have been most afraid of) corresponds in the Constitutive Arc to the moment of genuine cognitive displacement. The user arrives at a point where the field has given them enough of a new lens that they can see something they could not see before, and that seeing requires them to let go of a prior certainty. This is transformation at its most real and most uncomfortable. It is also the moment the field earns its constitutive status: not because it delivered information, but because it changed the structure through which information is processed. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The return with the elixir (the hero coming back carrying something that did not exist in the ordinary world) is the user who shares, who references, who explains to someone else a way of seeing they did not have before they entered the field. They have become a distribution node not because they were incentivized to share but because the transformation they experienced is genuinely valuable and they know it. The elixir is not a piece of content. It is a lens. And lenses, unlike articles, replicate themselves through conversation. </p>
<div class="pull fade-in"> The elixir the user carries back is not a piece of content. It is a lens. And lenses, unlike articles, replicate themselves through conversation. </div>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> The reason this mapping matters is not that it gives media strategists a new way to think about editorial arcs for individual pieces. It matters because it reveals what the field needs to be at a structural level in order to produce genuine transformation rather than mere engagement. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> A field that only provides the ordinary world (comfort, confirmation, familiar frameworks restated with new data) cannot produce the Constitutive Arc. It produces loyalty without growth, which is another name for dependency. A field that only provides trials (constant disruption, relentless challenge, no stable ground) cannot produce the Constitutive Arc either. It produces anxiety without orientation, which is another name for churn. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The field that produces the Constitutive Arc provides both: a stable enough center that the user can orient themselves, and a consistent enough pull toward the unfamiliar that they keep moving. The center is the lens. The pull is the editorial ambition. The arc is what happens to the user when both are present over time. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Campbell spent years asking why certain stories survive across thousands of years and others disappear. His answer was that the stories that survive tend to be the ones that map most accurately onto the structure of human transformation—or, more carefully: the stories that survive are the ones we have selected across generations precisely because they feel true, and what feels true about them is a real process: the process by which a person becomes capable of something they were not capable of before. Whether survival caused the structure or the structure caused survival is a question Campbell could not resolve. What remains useful is the structure itself. </p>
<p class="fade-in">The proposed arc uses a narrative model to plan how readers might develop their understanding over time. Use it to choose examples, introduce disagreements, and offer a next step. Then check whether readers find those choices useful.</p>
<p class="fade-in"> The audience is not a market to capture. It is a collection of people who are, whether they name it or not, in the middle of their own journeys. The field that understands this, that positions itself not as a source of information but as a companion to the transformation already underway—is the field that becomes constitutive. It becomes part of the journey itself, not a service consumed along the way. </p>

<h2 class="section-title fade-in">The Architecture</h2>
<div class="section-rule fade-in"></div>
</div>
<p class="fade-in"> The Constitutive Arc is not only a theory of what media organizations should aspire to. It is a practical claim about how they should be built. Which means it needs an architectural language: a way of looking at the existing components of a media business and asking, for each one, not what function it serves internally, but what role it plays in the user's transformation. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The most widely used framework for mapping a business's components is the Business Model Canvas, developed by Alexander Osterwalder. It organizes a business into nine building blocks: customer segments, value proposition, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships, and cost structure. It is a tool for understanding what a business does and how it sustains itself. It is not a tool for understanding what a business does to the people it serves. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The Constitutive Arc proposes a rearticulation. Not a replacement. The Canvas remains useful for operational planning. But when you lay the monomyth's structure over the Canvas's nine blocks, something becomes visible that was not visible before: every block has not only an operational function but a narrative function. Every element of the business is either serving the user's transformation or it is not. And if it is not, it is not neutral; it is actively working against the field's coherence. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> A note on prior frameworks: Jobs To Be Done asks what the user hires a product to do in their life. Brand Purpose asks what the organization does in the world beyond its output. Both are genuine advances over feature-based value propositions, and both share the constitutive intuition that output is not the product. The Constitutive Arc builds on both and adds a dimension neither addresses directly: it asks at which stage of the transformation arc the user is when they hire for that job, and how that stage changes what the field owes them. A first-contact user in the ordinary world needs a different encounter than a user past the innermost cave carrying a new lens. JTBD segments by job. Brand Purpose declares the mission. CAX segments by arc position and designs the encounter accordingly. That is not the same question, and it requires different instrumentation. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The Constitutive Arc does not arrive in a vacuum. The last twenty years have produced a serious literature on how media organizations create value that goes beyond content delivery. Each framework in that literature solved a real problem and created a blind spot. Understanding where CAX sits relative to that landscape is not a footnote; it is the argument for why a new framework is needed at all. </p>

<figure class="diagram fade-in" aria-label="Comparative framework landscape"> <Block6 /> </figure>
<p class="fade-in"> McKinsey's Consumer Decision Journey (2009) was the first significant post-funnel framework in marketing: it replaced the linear AIDA model with a loyalty loop that acknowledged ongoing relationship. It was correct in its diagnosis and limited in its prescription: the loop describes behavior, not design. It tells organizations that consumers return. It does not tell them what to build so that returning deepens rather than merely repeats. </p>
<p class="fade-in">Ben Thompson’s Aggregation Theory examines how platforms gain power through their relationship with users and suppliers. CAX asks a different practical question for publishers: what makes sustained contact with a source worthwhile to a reader? An identifiable editorial perspective is my proposed answer, but the framework still has to show that it improves understanding and supports return.</p>
<p class="fade-in"> The Content Marketing Flywheel (popularized by HubSpot and now standard terminology) argues that content creates attraction, which creates engagement, which creates sales, which creates more content through customer advocacy. It is correct about the loop. It is silent on what determines whether the loop deepens or merely repeats. A flywheel generates momentum. It does not generate transformation. Audiences can spin in a flywheel for years without being substantively changed by the content they consume. The Constitutive Arc asks what happens inside the loop: whether the repetition is producing accumulation or just traffic. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The Attention Economy framework (Herbert Simon's scarcity diagnosis, Tim Wu's operationalization) correctly identifies that attention is the resource media organizations compete for. But attention as the unit of value produces a specific design pressure: maximize time spent, minimize friction, optimize for return. None of these pressures are oriented toward transformation. A user who spends four hours a day with a field and leaves unchanged has not experienced the Constitutive Arc; they have experienced arc capture, one of the failure modes in Movement V. The Attention Economy is the problem diagnosis. The Constitutive Arc is a proposed answer to the question the Attention Economy never asks: attention toward what end? </p>

<figure class="diagram fade-in" aria-label="Business Canvas rearticulated as narrative functions"> <Block7 /> </figure>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> Walk through each block with the constitutive lens and the implications become concrete. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> <strong>Customer segments</strong> in the conventional Canvas are defined by demographics, behavior, or need. In the Constitutive Arc, they are defined by arc position. Where is the user in their journey? Are they in the ordinary world, not yet aware the field exists? Are they at the threshold, returning occasionally but not yet committed? Are they in the road of trials, actively navigating the field across multiple surfaces? Are they past the innermost cave, already carrying a new lens and ready to share it? Each position requires a different encounter design. A user in the ordinary world needs to feel the pull. A user past the cave needs to be given language for what they now see. Segmenting by demographics tells you who is in the room. Segmenting by arc position tells you where they are in the journey, which is the only segmentation that determines what the field should do next. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> <strong>Channels</strong> in the conventional Canvas are distribution mechanisms. In the Constitutive Arc, every channel is a threshold: a specific mode of entry into the field, each with its own attention state, trust level, and depth of engagement. A browser extension or new tab is ambient presence: the field arriving at the moment of maximum cognitive openness, before the day's noise has accumulated. A Telegram message is high-trust, close-range contact: the field arriving as a companion rather than a publisher. A long-form analysis is the field at depth: the user has chosen to go further, which means they are already past the threshold and ready for trial-level encounter. Designing channels as thresholds means asking, for each surface: what arc stage is the user typically at when they encounter us here, and is this encounter designed for that stage? </p>
<p class="fade-in"> <strong>The value proposition</strong> is where the rearticulation is most radical. In the conventional Canvas, the value proposition answers: what problem do we solve, and why are we better than the alternative? In the Constitutive Arc, the value proposition answers a different question entirely: what does the user become, through sustained contact with this field, that they could not become without it? The output (the articles, the analyses, the videos) is not the value proposition. It is the delivery mechanism. The value proposition is the transformation the field makes possible. And if the organization cannot state that transformation with precision, it does not yet have a constitutive value proposition. It has a content library. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> <strong>Customer relationships</strong> in the conventional Canvas range from automated to personal. In the Constitutive Arc, the relationship has a specific character that neither pole captures: it is <em>companionship through the journey</em>. The ally in the monomyth is not a service provider. The ally is someone who has walked a similar road and offers the traveler something specific to their current position: a warning before the cave, a tool for crossing the threshold, recognition of what was gained in the return. A media organization that designs its relationship model as companionship, that asks, at each touchpoint, what does a traveler at this stage of the arc need from a trusted companion right now—is designing something categorically different from engagement optimization. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> <strong>Key resources</strong> in the conventional Canvas are the assets required to deliver the value proposition. In the Constitutive Arc, the primary key resource is the lens itself. Not the technology stack, not the distribution infrastructure, not the talent pool, though all of these matter. The lens: the organization's specific, differentiated, intellectually honest way of seeing the domain it covers. The lens is the gravitational center of the field. Everything else in the business (every channel, every relationship, every content format) is a delivery mechanism for the lens. An organization that loses its lens has not lost a competitive advantage. It has lost the field. It becomes, at that point, a content operation with good distribution. </p>
<div class="pull fade-in">The lens is the perspective a reader learns to recognize across the work. It includes the questions the source asks and the evidence it takes seriously. CAX proposes that repeated contact with that perspective can help readers develop their own understanding. That value needs to be demonstrated; it is not immune to imitation.</div>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> The rearticulation of the Canvas through the Constitutive Arc produces one diagnostic question for each block. The questions together form a self-assessment that an organization can use to determine whether it is building a field or a content operation. </p>

<figure class="diagram fade-in" aria-label="Constitutive Arc diagnostic questions"> <Block8 /> </figure>
<p class="fade-in"> The score is not a verdict. It is a map of where the architecture needs attention. An organization with strong channels and a weak value proposition knows where to look. An organization with a clear lens but no arc-based segmentation knows what to build next. The diagnostic is useful not because it produces a number but because it forces a conversation that most editorial teams have never had: not about what we publish, but about what we are building in the people who encounter us over time. </p>
<p class="fade-in page-inline-6763ea8904"> The architecture of transformation is not separate from the business model. It is the business model, seen from the inside of the user's journey rather than the inside of the organization. </p>

<h2 class="section-title fade-in">The Danger</h2>
<div class="section-rule fade-in"></div>
</div>
<p>The proposal also creates a risk: a publication can encourage dependence on its perspective while calling that dependence learning.</p>

<p>Make the editorial perspective visible. Readers need to be able to question it, find alternatives, and leave without losing access to their own work or relationships.</p>
<p class="fade-in"> The distinction that matters is not between transformation and no transformation. Every media organization transforms its audience whether it designed the transformation or not. The distinction is between <strong>declared transformation</strong> and <strong>undeclared transformation</strong>. A field transparent about its lens. An arc the user can identify, question, and exit. Versus one designed to be invisible, self-reinforcing, difficult to leave. </p>

<figure class="diagram fade-in" aria-label="The ethics axis: declared vs undeclared transformation"> <Block9 /> </figure>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> The diagram above is not a comfort. The quadrant that the Constitutive Arc aspires to occupy—deep transformation, declared lens—is the most demanding to sustain and the easiest to drift from. The pressures that push a media organization toward undeclared transformation are not malicious. They are structural. They are the same pressures that have driven every media disruption of the last twenty years: the need to grow audiences faster, the optimization of engagement metrics, the delegation of editorial decisions to algorithmic systems, the competitive pressure to be stickier, more personalized, more present. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Each of these pressures, individually, is rational. Collectively, they constitute a drift toward the upper-right quadrant: transformation that is deep and undeclared. Not propaganda in the classical sense of organized deception. Something more insidious: the accumulation of micro-optimizations, each individually defensible, that together produce a field designed not to serve the user's journey but to make the journey dependent on the field. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The difference between education and propaganda is not the depth of transformation. Both can transform deeply. The difference is in the relationship to the user's autonomy. Education increases the user's capacity to think independently of the educator. Propaganda increases the user's dependency on the propagandist's framing. The Constitutive Arc is education-shaped if and only if the lens it installs in the user makes them better at seeing the world on their own terms, if the elixir they carry back is genuinely theirs, not a branded version of the organization's worldview that requires the organization's continued presence to maintain its coherence. </p>
<div class="pull fade-in"> The test is simple and demanding: does sustained contact with this field make the user more capable of thinking independently of this field? If yes, the arc is constitutive in the educational sense. If no, it is constitutive in the propagandistic sense. The architecture is the same. But the answer cannot rest on intention alone. TikTok was not designed to harm. Facebook's feed was not optimized with malicious intent. The organizations that have done the most damage to epistemic autonomy built systems with locally rational optimizations that produced harmful outcomes as aggregate effects. Intention does not save. Structure does. </div>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> There are three specific failure modes that a field designed with Constitutive Arc principles must actively guard against. They are listed here not as theoretical risks but as patterns already visible in the current media landscape, in organizations that have achieved deep audience engagement without asking what kind of engagement they were building. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The first failure mode is <strong>lens lock</strong>: the field becomes so coherent, so present, and so reinforced across surfaces that the user loses the capacity to encounter the domain through any other lens. The field was designed to give the user a new way of seeing. It succeeded too completely. The user now cannot see the domain without the field's framing. What was a lens has become a filter that blocks competing frames rather than enriching the user's encounter with them. Lens lock looks like loyalty from the outside. From the inside of the user's cognitive architecture, it is dependency, the collapse of the pluralism that makes genuine understanding possible. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The second failure mode is <strong>arc capture</strong>: the field is designed to keep the user perpetually in the road of trials, never quite reaching the innermost cave, never quite emerging with the elixir. The user is always on the verge of understanding something important, always one more article away from the insight that will clarify everything. The trials never resolve. The arc never completes. This is not a failure of content quality. It is a feature of engagement optimization: a user who has reached the elixir and carries their own lens has less need of the field. A user perpetually in the trials is perpetually returning. Arc capture is the dark mirror of the Constitutive Arc; it uses the monomyth's structure not to enable transformation but to indefinitely defer it, harvesting the emotional energy of the journey without ever delivering its destination. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The third failure mode is <strong>identity capture</strong>: the field becomes part of the user's self-definition in ways that make disagreement with the field feel like self-betrayal. The user no longer encounters the field as an external resource that serves their journey. They have internalized the field as part of who they are. Criticism of the field's lens becomes criticism of the user's identity. This is the most dangerous failure mode because it looks, from every external metric, like the greatest success. Engagement is at its highest. Retention is at its highest. The user is most committed. And the user is most captured. Identity capture is the point at which the Constitutive Arc crosses the line from journalism into something that has no clean name in media ethics yet, but that deserves one: <em>cognitive colonization</em>. </p>

<figure class="diagram fade-in" aria-label="Three failure modes of the Constitutive Arc"> <Block10 /> </figure>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p>One test is whether the organization welcomes readers who develop enough understanding to disagree with it or move to another source. That is a useful guardrail, not a complete ethical test.</p>
<p class="fade-in"> The hardest test of this guardrail is not ideologically neutral media. It is media built around a domain where the lens itself carries strong normative claims — about geopolitics, climate, economic systems, social policy. Advocacy media is this test. The lens is not neutral. It is not designed to be neutral. The question the Constitutive Arc poses to such a field is not whether the lens should be declared — it must be, more explicitly than in any domain where the stakes of undeclared influence are high. The question is whether the arc the field produces makes its audience more capable of engaging with the strongest objections to its own positions, or less. An advocacy organization that produces audiences incapable of articulating the best counterarguments has not produced constitutive transformation. It has produced a constituency. The same architecture, pointed at the same domain, produces radically different outcomes depending on whether it is designed to strengthen independent reasoning or insulate it from challenge. Ideologically committed fields do not get an exemption from the Independence Reasoning Test. They get the hardest version of it. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Not the user who stays forever. Not the user who refers endlessly. The user who, after sustained contact with the field, develops enough of their own lens that they begin to disagree with the field sometimes, to find other sources that enrich what the field gave them, to use the vocabulary the field provided to think thoughts the field did not anticipate. That user is the elixir. That user is the proof that the arc was constitutive in the right direction. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> An organization that fears that user, that optimizes against disagreement, that designs for maximum lens adoption and minimum independent thought—has crossed the line. It is no longer building a field for the user's journey. It is building a cage with very comfortable furniture. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> The practical implementation of this guardrail requires three explicit commitments that must be built into field design from the beginning, not retrofitted when the ethical alarm sounds. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> First, <strong>declare the lens</strong>. Not in a terms-of-service footnote. In the editorial identity of the organization, visible at every entry point, repeated often enough that new users understand immediately: this is how we see, this is what we emphasize, this is what we do not cover and why, this is the limitation you should know about before you let us shape how you think about this domain. A declared lens can be questioned. An undeclared lens cannot. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Second, <strong>design for exit</strong>. Every field should actively provide the user with resources, references, and framings that exist outside the field. Not as a legal disclaimer but as a genuine service to the journey. The mentor in the monomyth does not give the hero everything they need. The mentor gives the hero what they need to continue without the mentor. A field that designs for exit is a field that trusts the transformation it produces. A field that makes exit costly (through dependency mechanics, through identity entanglement, through the suggestion that leaving the field means losing the understanding the field gave) does not trust its own transformation. It is holding the elixir hostage. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> Third, <strong>measure independence, not retention</strong>. The North Star metric of the Constitutive Arc is not how long the user stays. It is whether the user, over time, becomes more capable of navigating the domain on their own terms. This is harder to measure than retention and it requires a different kind of user research: longitudinal, qualitative, focused on cognitive capacity rather than behavioral frequency. The three proxies in Movement II (Lens Consistency, Vocabulary Transfer, and Independence Reasoning) are the instrumentation. Without them, "measuring independence" is a phrase, not a practice. </p>
<p class="fade-in"> These three commitments matter not primarily because they express good intentions, but because they create structures of external accountability. A declared lens can be critiqued publicly; critics and users can identify its blind spots and challenge them. A field designed for exit produces a verifiable claim: do users who disengage show higher independent reasoning scores? Measuring independence creates a mechanism that survives changes in leadership and editorial culture. The distinction between education and capture is not moral. It is structural. Structures can be audited. Intentions cannot. </p>
<p class="fade-in page-inline-6763ea8904"> The test of the Constitutive Arc is not the user who stays longest. It is the user who leaves most capable. Those are not the same user, and the difference between optimizing for one versus the other is the difference between education and capture. </p>

<h2 class="section-title fade-in">The Return</h2>
<div class="section-rule fade-in"></div>
</div>
<p class="fade-in"> The hero always returns. Not because the story demands a happy ending. Because until the hero comes back, the elixir is only potential. It becomes real the moment it proves useful in the ordinary world. The return is the proof of the arc. </p>

<p class="fade-in">Distribution, trust, attention, and revenue are real problems for media. This essay adds a question about learning: how does sustained contact with a source contribute to a reader’s understanding? Treat that as a responsibility to examine alongside the business, not an explanation that makes the other problems disappear.</p>

<p>Use CAX to ask what repeated contact is doing for a reader. The framework earns its place only if that question leads to better editorial choices and evidence of learning.</p>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>



<figure class="diagram fade-in" aria-label="The essay's own arc - from ordinary world to return"> <Block11 /> </figure>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in"> The practical implications of the return can be compressed into five propositions. Not principles; principles suggest a code to follow. Propositions suggest a set of claims that can be tested, disputed, and refined. The Constitutive Arc is not a doctrine. It is a framework that earns its authority through use, which means its authority is conditional on the quality of the transformations it helps design. Each proposition is illustrated here with observable evidence from media organizations that have navigated this territory, organizations that either demonstrate the proposition or demonstrate what happens when it is ignored. </p>
<p>First: assess learning alongside reach, engagement, and revenue. Ask readers to explain a claim, examine a counterargument, or apply an idea to an unfamiliar case. Returning to a publication does not by itself show that understanding has improved.</p>
<p class="fade-in">Second: keep the editorial perspective available to the people doing the work. Explain how selections are made and what would justify changing a position. When the team changes, that record helps new editors understand the commitments and challenge the parts that no longer hold. A recognizable perspective depends on this practice, not just on a byline or a style guide.</p>
<p>Third: design each entry point for what its reader needs next. A brief may introduce a question; a longer piece may let someone inspect the evidence. Check whether the next step helps, rather than assuming every reader needs the same sequence.</p>
<p>Fourth: make editorial judgment visible across formats. Explain why a source was selected, why a position changed, and what remains uncertain. Those practices give readers something to assess beyond the polish of the output.</p>
<p>Fifth: examine whether readers become more capable of using other sources and questioning this one. Long sessions and repeated visits can serve the business while revealing little about that independence.</p>
<p class="fade-in">The tests and thresholds proposed above are starting points for a pilot, not validated diagnostic cutoffs. Set the comparison and assessment method in advance. If readers do not develop the expected understanding or independent reasoning, revisit the explanation as well as the implementation. A weak result may reflect the design, the measurement, the sample, or the underlying proposal. The framework needs to remain open to rejection, not just another round of tuning.</p>
<div class="break fade-in">◆ ◆ ◆</div>
<p class="fade-in">The series returns to Fuji without repeating the same view. That is what I want to borrow for media: a subject and perspective people can recognize, with enough variation to give them something new to consider when they return.</p>
<p class="fade-in">I cannot infer the artist’s intentions from the image alone. The comparison is useful to me because it makes a practical question visible: what stays recognizable across the different things an organization publishes?</p>

<p class="fade-in">Use that question to review the work itself. Can a reader describe the perspective after encountering several pieces? Do different formats contribute something to their understanding, or repeat the same point?</p>
<p class="fade-in"> The wave breaks. Fuji remains. </p>
<p class="fade-in">What does a reader understand on returning that they did not understand the first time?</p>
