<div class="essay-section intro">
<p>Work doesn't disappear. It devalues.</p>
<div class="article-opening-diagram"><Block0 /></div>
<p>You notice it when the rate drops or a client hesitates. Work that once required a team may now be completed by fewer people with different tools. The price can change before a worker has time to adapt.</p>
<p>I see five costs worth separating: falling rates for existing skills, uneven judgment when using the tools, unclear responsibility, changes in relationships, and decisions about who sets the rules. The first four can consume the attention needed to influence the fifth.</p>
<p>I've been here before. Not here exactly (no one has been here exactly) but close enough to recognize the texture of what a transition costs when you're living inside it rather than watching it from the outside. I'm forty-three. My generation learned to work in one world and had to rebuild entirely for another. We watched our parents' knowledge become worthless. We built new skills on new ground and called it adaptation. What I didn't understand then, and do now, is that adaptation and paying the tax are the same thing.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<div class="levy-header">
<div class="levy-header-num">I</div>
<div class="levy-header-body">
<div class="levy-header-label">First Cost</div>
<div class="levy-header-title">Devaluation Without Announcement</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="margin-note">The places where devalued workers historically relocated are themselves being devalued before the relocation is complete.</div>
<p>What's different this time is not the mechanism. Devaluation is the oldest story in the economics of labor. What's different is the breadth and the speed of convergence.</p>
<p>Previous waves moved through industries sequentially: manufacturing, then clerical work, then routine professional services. Each wave gave adjacent sectors time to observe, adapt, and reposition before the pressure arrived at their specific position. Workers displaced from one category relocated to another that hadn't yet been touched.</p>
<p>This wave is arriving everywhere at once. Legal research and software development and financial analysis and strategic planning are being hit by the same force on the same timeline. The places where devalued workers historically relocated are themselves being devalued before the relocation is complete. The adjustment mechanism that previous transitions relied on is slower than the transition it is supposed to manage.</p>
<div class="pull-quote">
<p>The Transition Tax on this cost is paid in the gap between the skills you built and the skills the market now prices. Retraining takes time, and workers may face lower rates while they are still adapting. And time is the one resource this transition doesn't offer generously.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<div class="levy-header">
<div class="levy-header-num">II</div>
<div class="levy-header-body">
<div class="levy-header-label">Second Cost</div>
<div class="levy-header-title">The Criterion Gap</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="margin-note">The interface looks the same. The error rate is not.</div>
<p>By the end of this decade, having access to AI tools will confer roughly the same advantage as having access to a search engine: necessary but not differentiating. The tools will be cheap, available, and widely distributed. What won't distribute at the same rate is the ability to use them well.</p>
<p>The criterion gap is not about intelligence. It is about domain depth. A lawyer using AI to draft a contract needs to know enough law to recognize when the draft is subtly incorrect in ways that will matter in court. A doctor using AI for differential diagnosis needs to know enough medicine to catch the plausible-sounding error that a less experienced colleague would miss. A strategist using AI to analyze a market needs enough judgment to know when the synthesis is missing the variable that changes everything. The tool amplifies existing judgment. It does not replace judgment that isn't there.</p>
<p>AI makes strong practitioners dramatically more productive and weak practitioners dramatically more dangerous. The tool produces confident outputs regardless of whether the underlying judgment evaluating those outputs is sound. The interface looks the same. The error rate is not.</p>
<p>More capable AI produces more convincing errors. The criterion gap may widen before it narrows, because the outputs become harder to question precisely as they become more capable of being consequentially wrong. Access is a threshold problem. Criterion is a depth problem. Solving the first does not touch the second.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<div class="levy-header">
<div class="levy-header-num">III</div>
<div class="levy-header-body">
<div class="levy-header-label">Third Cost</div>
<div class="levy-header-title">The Responsibility Vacuum</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="margin-note">The resolution will come from legislation, eventually. But legislation moves in years. Agent capabilities move in months.</div>
<p>Every previous wave of automation operated within existing legal structures. Machines were owned. Software ran on servers that were owned. However complex the chain of causation when something went wrong, responsibility could eventually be traced back to an identifiable legal person.</p>
<p>Autonomous actions make the chain harder to inspect. A useful record needs to show what was authorized, what the system did, and who could intervene.</p>
<p>Consider what happens when an agent causes harm through a sequence of actions no single human authorized at each step. The developer who built the agent designed a system, not a decision. The operator who deployed it set parameters, not outcomes. The infrastructure provider whose platform the agent runs on has terms of service written specifically to exclude liability for what autonomous systems do on their infrastructure. There is no villain in this story. There is a gap where accountability used to be.</p>
<p>This gap is not theoretical. It is arriving now, in the space between what agents can already do and what legal frameworks exist to govern what they do. The resolution will come from legislation and jurisprudence, eventually, but those move on timescales measured in years and decades rather than in the months at which agent capabilities advance. In that gap, the costs of agent errors will be borne by whoever is least able to absorb them, not because anyone decided that was fair but because the structures that would distribute those costs equitably don't exist yet.</p>
<div class="pull-quote">
<p>When an automated system causes harm, the people affected need a clear route to the organization responsible for deploying it. The practical gap I mean here is an unclear chain of responsibility: who authorized the action, who could stop it, and who must respond when it fails?</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<div class="levy-header">
<div class="levy-header-num">IV</div>
<div class="levy-header-body">
<div class="levy-header-label">Fourth Cost</div>
<div class="levy-header-title">The Fraying of Social Texture</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="margin-note">Drift is what happens when choice is never quite made.</div>
<p>The first three costs show up in market rates, court filings, and audit reports. This one shows up in nothing you can measure until it's too late to reverse.</p>
<p>When you can have a conversation with a system that is patient, never tired, and available at any hour, that changes more than your habits. It changes your tolerance for the friction that human relationships require. Human relationships require friction. They require the experience of someone not understanding you immediately, of having to find different words, of waiting, of the other person having their own needs that don't align with yours at this particular moment.</p>
<p>That friction is not a flaw in human connection. It is the mechanism by which trust is built and genuine understanding develops. The capacity to sustain difficult relationships, to tolerate misunderstanding without retreating, to be present with another person through prolonged uncertainty: these are not things you lose and then retrain. They build through cumulative exposure to exactly the situations that frictionless systems are designed to eliminate.</p>
<p>Think about the last time someone you loved didn't understand what you were trying to say and you had to find a different way to say it, and in finding it you understood something about what you actually meant. That work is what's being priced out of the market for human attention. Not replaced. Priced out. The lower-friction option is always available. Most people will take it, most of the time, without deciding to. That is what drift is.</p>
<p>My parents' generation lost certain practical knowledge when the world industrialized around them. Things you did with your hands, with neighbors, with time. My generation lost something subtler when work went digital and remote: reading a room, negotiating in person, being physically present with people you disagreed with until you found something workable. We didn't notice we were losing it because we were gaining something else. The next generation is losing something more intimate still, and they won't notice either, because the system offering the replacement is patient and available and never makes them feel stupid for needing to ask again.</p>
<p>The loss is not visible until you try to draw on the resource and find it wasn't built.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<div class="levy-header">
<div class="levy-header-num">V</div>
<div class="levy-header-body">
<div class="levy-header-label">Fifth Cost</div>
<div class="levy-header-title">The Institutional Capture Problem</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="margin-note">Standards become invisible precisely because everything is built on top of them.</div>
<p>The four costs above fall on individuals. This one falls on societies, and it may be costly to reverse.</p>
<p>Choices about authorization, attribution, oversight, and recovery are becoming part of the systems organizations adopt. There is already governance work to build on: <a href="https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-ai-rmf-10">NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework</a> includes responsibilities and accountability in managing AI risk. My concern is whether affected workers and users can influence how those principles become operating decisions.</p>
<p>Technical standards can embed choices that later become difficult to revisit. Who may authorize an action? What evidence is retained? Who can contest a result? Those questions deserve discussion before an organization builds its workflows around the answers.</p>
<p>This is the mechanism that makes the fifth cost different from the first four. The devaluation of skills can be addressed by developing new skills. The criterion gap can be closed through education and experience. Responsibility requires explicit rules, enforceable obligations, and a usable process for resolving disputes. The fraying of social texture responds to deliberate choices about how to spend time and attention.</p>
<p>Institutional capture, once complete, requires dismantling infrastructure. That is a different category of problem.</p>
<p>There is a name for this at the individual scale: Sovereignty Debt. It accumulates when you build your audience, your business, or your professional identity on platforms whose rules you don't control. You discover the cost at the moment you try to act from your own priorities and find the architecture was never designed to permit it. The person who built a following on a platform that changed its algorithm, the business that built on an API that changed its terms, the country that adopted technical standards written by someone else: they all discovered the same thing in the same way. The infrastructure they depended on was never theirs.</p>
<div class="pull-quote">
<p>At the civilizational level, the same dynamic is playing out now, in real time, in rooms most people will never enter.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<div class="levy-header">
<div class="levy-header-num page-inline-be74fe6504">⊘</div>
<div class="levy-header-body">
<div class="levy-header-label">On Why the Fifth Cost Is the Hardest to See</div>
<div class="levy-header-title">The Interaction Effect</div>
</div>
</div>
<p>The five costs are not independent. The first creates immediate financial pressure. The second creates invisible performance risk that looks like capability until it produces a catastrophic error. The third creates a legal environment where harm is distributed downward by default. The fourth creates social conditions where the capacity for sustained collective attention is quietly eroding.</p>
<p>Each of these is louder than the fifth. Each demands to be addressed first. And the fifth cost (the one about who is writing the rules of the agentic world while everyone else is managing the disruption of the first four) benefits from exactly that noise.</p>
<p>Institutional capture doesn't require conspiracy. It requires that the people who understand what's being decided have the time and attention to show up, and that the others don't.</p>

</div>
<div class="closing">
<div class="closing-ornament-top">✦ ❧ ✦</div>
<p>Somewhere right now, a working group is making a decision about agent liability that will look, in fifteen years, like a natural feature of the legal landscape. No one in that room thinks they are writing the constitution of a new era. They think they are solving a technical problem under deadline.</p>
<p>Technical standards can embed choices that later become difficult to revisit.</p>
<p>Make time to review authorization, evidence, and appeals while the workflow is still being designed. After adoption, changing those choices becomes harder.</p>
