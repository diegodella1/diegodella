<div class="arg-title">When the Customer Is Not the Decision-Maker</div>
<div class="arg-sub">AI agents buy things. What does marketing do now?</div>
<p>Every persuasion architecture built over the last hundred years assumes a human at the moment of decision. The billboard, the landing page, the brand story, the emotional hook, the social proof: all of it operates on human psychology at the exact moment a person considers whether to act. That assumption is dissolving. AI agents are beginning to execute purchasing decisions on behalf of humans who set criteria in advance and may never review the individual choice.</p>
<p>What does persuasion look like when the entity being persuaded has no emotions, carries no brand memories from childhood, and evaluates entirely against structured verifiable signals? The answer is not better SEO. It is a fundamentally different theory of why any organization deserves to be chosen, one built on verifiability rather than feeling, on track record rather than story, on machine-readable signals rather than human-felt associations.</p>
<p>The criteria a person gives an assistant may reflect earlier encounters with products and sources. That gives me a question for brand work: when and how were those criteria formed, and what would cause the person to revise them? The extent of that influence needs to be observed in actual decisions.</p>
<div class="arg-rupture">
<div class="arg-rupture-label">What breaks</div>
<div class="arg-rupture-text">Every brand investment made on the assumption that the buyer experiences the brand at the moment of purchase. Category leadership built on emotional resonance rather than verifiable differentiation. The entire apparatus of advertising designed to intercept attention at decision time, when there is no human attention left to intercept.</div>
</div>

<div class="arg-title">The Death of Taste as Signal</div>
<div class="arg-sub">When AI produces anything on demand, what does preference reveal?</div>
<p>Taste has always functioned as a signal of human interiority. What you prefer reveals something about who you are. Curators, editors, and critics derived authority precisely because their preferences were informative: hard to fake at scale and expensive to develop over time.</p>
<p>A system responding to stated preferences can narrow what a person encounters, but it can also offer unfamiliar examples or recommendations. My concern is what the particular system optimizes for. Does it help someone explore beyond a familiar pattern, or keep giving them variations of what previously held their attention? That is a design and measurement question, not an incapacity built into the definition of AI.</p>
<p>The argument forming: AI-mediated content discovery will produce a generation of people whose stated preferences become progressively more predictable, more self-referential, and less capable of genuine surprise. Not the death of culture. The calcification of individual preference into an ever-narrowing loop that feels increasingly satisfying and is actually increasingly shallow.</p>
<div class="arg-rupture">
<div class="arg-rupture-label">What breaks</div>
<div class="arg-rupture-text">The function of editors and curators as taste-developers rather than taste-satisfiers. The market for genuinely challenging work that does not optimize against existing preference. The mechanism by which culture renews itself through encounters people did not choose and could not have predicted they needed.</div>
</div>

<div class="arg-title">Memory and the Limits of Familiarity</div>
<div class="arg-sub">A system can retain context. What obligations should come with that continuity?</div>
<p>Human trust is built through accumulated experience over time. You trust a doctor because you have seen them be right before. You trust a brand because it has consistently delivered. You trust a friend because you share a history that gives both of you a stake in the relationship continuing. Trust is, at its core, a function of memory: the record of past interactions that makes future behavior predictable.</p>
<p>Some AI products retain information across interactions. For example, <a href="https://openai.com/index/memory-and-new-controls-for-chatgpt/">ChatGPT supports saved memories and reference to chat history</a>, depending on settings and availability. Retained context can create continuity, but it does not by itself establish the obligations of a reciprocal relationship. The question is what a user can reasonably expect the service and its operator to remember, protect, and take responsibility for.</p>
<p>The institutional question is who carries the relationship when AI mediates a service. A bank or advisory service may have an extensive record of a customer even if a particular model call does not. Users need to know what information is retained, where continuity breaks, and which person or organization can respond when the system gets something wrong.</p>
<div class="arg-rupture">
<div class="arg-rupture-label">What breaks</div>
<div class="arg-rupture-text">The assumption that familiarity and trust are the same thing. The institutions that have substituted AI efficiency for human relationship without understanding what the relationship was actually doing. And the people who mistake the feeling of being understood for actually being understood, which may be the defining psychological confusion of the next decade.</div>
</div>

<div class="arg-title">The Collapse of Effort as Proof</div>
<div class="arg-sub">When anyone can produce anything instantly, what does quality signal?</div>
<p>Before AI, the quality of a produced thing carried implicit information about the effort behind it. A well-written essay suggested careful thought. A polished design suggested developed skill. A comprehensive research report suggested significant time invested. Quality was, among other things, a proxy for investment, and investment was a signal of commitment, care, and competence.</p>
<p>AI breaks this proxy completely. High production quality no longer implies high investment of human time or skill. The essay, the design, the report: all of it can be produced in minutes by someone with zero relevant expertise. The signal that quality carried has been decoupled from the thing quality was signaling. The result is not just that evaluating work becomes harder. It is that the entire framework through which humans have assessed credibility based on observable output needs to be rebuilt from scratch.</p>
<p>What replaces effort as proof? The forming hypothesis: consequence replaces effort. What someone is willing to put their name on when they could hide behind generation. What someone will defend when challenged. What someone has a visible stake in being right about. Consequentiality, not quality of output but cost of being wrong, becomes the credibility signal that effort used to provide. That is a profound restructuring of how authority is earned in almost every domain.</p>
<div class="arg-rupture">
<div class="arg-rupture-label">What breaks</div>
<div class="arg-rupture-text">Every evaluation system that uses quality of output as a proxy for human competence: job hiring, academic assessment, investment due diligence, deciding who to listen to. The infrastructure of credentialing, which exists to certify that the person behind the output earned the right to produce it. And the social contract that effort deserves recognition, when effort is no longer distinguishable from its absence.</div>

<div class="arg-title">Intelligence Without Skin in the Game</div>
<div class="arg-sub">Who is responsible when automated advice is wrong?</div>
<p>Nassim Taleb's argument about skin in the game is essentially about asymmetry: advice from someone who bears no cost of being wrong is structurally corrupted, regardless of the intelligence of the advisor. The doctor who recommends surgery but does not perform it. The consultant who recommends restructuring but does not manage the team through it. The advisor who recommends the investment but does not hold the position. In each case the intelligence may be genuine, the advice technically correct, and yet something is missing that no amount of better analysis compensates for.</p>
<p>A model does not bear personal stakes in its advice. The organization providing or deploying it can still bear responsibility and reputational consequences. The practical question is whether those obligations are clear to the user and whether there is a usable process for correcting or contesting a result.</p>
<p>As AI becomes the default advisor in more consequential domains, the absence of skin in the game stops being abstract and becomes a practical problem with measurable consequences. Not because the AI is malicious. Because consequentiality is what calibrates advice-giving over time. Without it, even excellent intelligence drifts in ways that are invisible until the cost accumulates into something that cannot be ignored.</p>
<div class="arg-rupture">
<div class="arg-rupture-label">What breaks</div>
<div class="arg-rupture-text">The assumption that intelligence plus information is sufficient for good advice. The institutional structures that used professional accountability as the mechanism for aligning advisor and advisee: those structures were not arbitrary, they were solving the skin-in-the-game problem that AI reintroduces at scale. And potentially: the collective ability to distinguish between advice that is analytically correct and advice that is actually actionable for the specific person receiving it.</div>

<div class="arg-title">The New Scarcity</div>
<div class="arg-sub">If AI can produce almost anything, what becomes expensive?</div>
<p>Economics is the study of scarcity. When something becomes abundant it loses value. When something becomes scarce it gains it. AI is making previously scarce things abundant at extraordinary speed: writing, code, images, translation, analysis, synthesis, customer service, legal drafts, medical explanations. The economic value of those things is falling and will continue to fall. That part is not controversial.</p>
<p>What is genuinely open: what becomes newly scarce as a result? Scarcity does not disappear. It migrates. The printing press made handwritten manuscripts obsolete and made literacy newly valuable. Photography made painted portraiture obsolete and made the commissioned portrait newly precious as a signal of status. Every technology that makes something abundant creates new scarcity somewhere else. What is the analogue for AI?</p>
<p>I am considering deliberate human attention, visible responsibility for errors, physical presence, and opportunities to work through uncertainty. Each deserves a separate argument. Their value will depend on how people use the systems and organize the services around them, not on a definition that makes AI incapable of contributing.</p>
<div class="arg-rupture">
<div class="arg-rupture-label">Why this stays open</div>
<div class="arg-rupture-text">The list of new scarcities is still forming. The economic mechanisms that will price them are not yet visible. And the answer is probably domain-specific in ways that make any general claim misleading. This is the argument that needs the most time before it becomes a paper, and the one whose answer matters most for every strategic decision being made right now.</div>
</div>

<div class="arg-title">The Tastemaker Premium</div>
<div class="arg-sub">When AI satisfies preference at scale, the person who can surprise you becomes unreasonably powerful</div>
<p>Argument 2 on this page describes the death of taste as signal. This argument runs in the opposite direction and both possibilities are worth examining together. That tension is the point.</p>
<p>When AI produces infinite content calibrated to stated preference, it creates two populations. The majority get what they asked for, efficiently and continuously. Their preference loop closes. They are satisfied in a way that makes them progressively less capable of being surprised and progressively less interested in trying. That population is large, growing, and economically important to AI companies building for scale.</p>
<p>Some people may seek a curator because they want something outside their familiar preferences. A record of useful, unexpected selections could make that relationship valuable. It remains to be seen how that value compares with recommendations from systems designed to encourage exploration.</p>
<p>The historical parallel is the concert after the phonograph. Before recorded music, all music was live and undifferentiated. After, recorded music became the commodity and the live performance became a categorically distinct experience with its own economy, its own pricing logic, its own meaning. The artist did not disappear. The artist became something different and more expensive precisely because mechanical reproduction created an abundance that live presence could never be part of. AI does the same thing to curation that the phonograph did to performance. The tastemaker does not compete with the abundance. They become the proof that the abundance is not enough.</p>
<div class="arg-rupture">
<div class="arg-rupture-label">What this creates</div>
<div class="arg-rupture-text">A stratification of cultural authority sharper than anything that existed before. The tastemakers who survive the transition, those with genuine perspective, verifiable track record, and the specific kind of authority that comes from having been right before the consensus, will hold more power over the fraction of the population that still wants to be surprised than the most followed curator in history ever held over their audience. Because the contrast with the alternative has never been this stark, and contrast is what makes scarcity legible.</div>
