<div class="section-number">Section I / Four</div>
<h2 class="section-title">The Drift Problem</h2>
</div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>A new hire asks why the product works this way. The people who made the decision remember; everyone else has to guess. As the team grows, those guesses start shaping the product.</p>
<div class="article-opening-diagram"><Block0 /></div>
<p>At launch, the people who built the product are still the people running it. They remember the original argument. They know why certain decisions were made and what they were protecting. The narrative is not written down anywhere formal, but it does not need to be; it lives in the room, in the people who were there when the story was agreed on.</p>
<div class="margin-note">The narrative does not need documentation when everyone who agreed on it is still in the room. It needs documentation the moment the first person who wasn't there joins.</div>
<p>Then the company grows. New hires arrive who were never part of that original conversation. Priorities shift under the pressure of quarterly targets. Features get added not because they serve the claim but because a competitor shipped them, or because a large customer requested them, or because someone in a roadmap meeting made a compelling case that was technically correct and narratively incoherent. The drift may pass unnoticed if the team has no regular way to review those choices. The original story is not violated in a single dramatic moment. It is diluted, one reasonable-sounding decision at a time.</p>
<div class="pull-quote">
<p>This is what I call Narrative Drift. It is a gradual loss of clarity that can become expensive to reverse.</p>
</div>
<p>By the time the product feels incoherent to the market, the decisions that caused it are two years old and buried under layers of subsequent choices that each made sense in isolation. The organization is not failing. It is succeeding at the wrong thing, consistently, over time.</p>
</div>
</article>

<article class="section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"> <Block2 /> </div>
<div class="section-meta">
<div class="section-number">Section II / Four</div>
<h2 class="section-title">Coherence as Economic Moat</h2>
</div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">

<p>A consistent product gives users a clearer idea of what to expect from the next release. That history can help a company introduce new work, but it does not guarantee lower acquisition costs or a successful launch. The new experience still needs to keep the promise people recognize.</p>
<p>This is the economic moat of coherence. And unlike most moats (network effects, proprietary data, regulatory capture), it is one that a small, disciplined team can build from the beginning. It does not require scale to generate. It requires only the sustained commitment to protect the original claim against the entropy of growth.</p>
<div class="pull-quote">
<p>Growth creates pressure to accept exceptions. Record which commitment each exception changes, so the next team can see the tradeoff.</p>
</div>
</div>
</article>

<article class="section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"> <Block3 /> </div>
<div class="section-meta">
<div class="section-number">Section III / Four</div>
<h2 class="section-title">Narrative Governance</h2>
</div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>Review the product’s commitments where decisions happen: roadmap discussions, partnerships, and changes in audience. A style guide cannot resolve a disagreement about what the product should do.</p>
<p>Narrative governance, done well, is something quieter. It is the set of decision-making habits that keeps the product aligned with its original claim without requiring constant oversight. And it has a specific starting point: the Operating Narrative cannot be a founding artifact. It has to be a living one.</p>
<div class="margin-note">Keep the Operating Narrative in use: review it when the product or audience changes.</div>
<p>Most organizations write their version of the Operating Narrative once. It might be a pitch deck, a launch post, a company manifesto. They treat it as a record of how things began rather than as the active document that governs how things evolve. The problem is that a narrative written for eight people does not automatically serve eighty. The claim may hold, but the constraints change. The Technical Truths need to be renegotiated as the product grows. The Recognition Moment needs to be re-examined as the user base shifts. If no one is doing that work, the Operating Narrative becomes a relic; something people cite in onboarding decks but no longer use to make decisions. And the moment it stops being used to make decisions, the Drift has already begun.</p>
<p>The second habit is making the original claim the explicit test for new decisions; not as a veto mechanism, but as a question that is asked early enough to matter. Before a feature goes on the roadmap, before a partnership is signed, before a new market is entered, someone needs to ask whether this move strengthens or weakens what the product is claiming to be. That question does not always produce a no. Sometimes it produces a better yes; a version of the idea that serves both the commercial objective and the narrative integrity. But it has to arrive before the decision is made, not as a justification constructed afterward.</p>
<p>The third habit is the most human one and the hardest to systematize: keeping the original story alive in the room. The people who join in year three have no memory of the founding argument. They have the product as it currently exists and the culture as they encounter it. If neither of those things transmits the original claim clearly, what replaces it is not a better story. It is a collection of priorities without a center. Narrative governance is, at its core, a transmission problem. The question is not whether the story exists. It is whether the organization can still tell it to itself.</p>
</div>
</article>

<p>The pattern underneath all four of these essays is the same one. Strategic Debt at launch. Narrative Drift at scale. Different names for the same error; clarity deferred, compounding quietly, discovered late.</p>
<p>The companies I have seen sustain coherence over time are not the ones who never faced pressure to compromise. They all did. They are the ones who had a clear enough articulation of their original claim that they could evaluate the compromise before accepting it. They knew what they were trading, and they made the trade consciously rather than by default. That distinction (between a conscious trade and a default one) is the difference between a product that evolves and one that erodes.</p>
<p>This is what the framework is ultimately for. Not to make every decision perfect. Not to eliminate the tension between short-term commercial pressure and long-term narrative integrity. That tension is real and it does not go away. The framework exists to make the tension visible early enough to manage it deliberately.</p>
<p>The products that age well are not the ones that never changed. They are the ones that changed in ways that confirmed rather than contradicted what they had always been about. Their users felt the evolution as growth, not as drift. And that feeling (of a product that knows what it is, even as it becomes more of it) is the rarest and most valuable thing a company can build.</p>
<p>It does not start at scale. It does not start at launch.</p>
<p>It starts before the first sprint, in the moment someone slows down long enough to ask the question this series began with: what is the story, exactly, and is it worth building?</p>
<p>Keep that answer available to the people making the next decision.</p>
