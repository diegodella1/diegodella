<div class="essay-section intro">
<p>What interests me is the experience behind the work: the decisions someone made, the things they tried, and the questions they still cannot settle.</p>
<div class="article-opening-diagram"><Block0 /></div>
<p>I use origin to name that history: someone worked through a question under particular conditions and had something at stake in the answer.</p>
<p>An assistant can summarize an argument or imitate aspects of its presentation. What I want from the source is also an account of how the person reached it: the situation, the choices, and the things they would now do differently. That interest in the source is the relationship this essay tries to describe.</p>
</div>
<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block1 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 01</span> <h2 class="section-title">What Origin Is</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>By origin, I mean the particular work and circumstances behind an argument. You do not need to publish your private life to describe them. A decision, a failed attempt, or an unexpected result can give a reader something concrete to question.</p>
<p>You recognize it as a reader when you find it. Not as a credential. As a pressure. The way a piece weights certain things and not others. What it treats as obvious versus what it treats as worth explaining. The angle of entry into the problem. These are traces of a standpoint, and the standpoint is not in the text. It is in the person. The text is what the standpoint produced, but the two are not the same thing. An agent trained on that text inherits the conclusions. The standpoint stays behind.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block2 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 02</span> <h2 class="section-title">Why Gravity</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>Gravity because it operates below the level of decision. You don't choose to feel it. You notice, after the fact, that you went looking for the source. The agent gave you the answer, the answer was correct, and you still opened another tab. You followed someone for two years not because every post was good but because there was a mind on the other end and you wanted to stay near it.</p>
<p>A summary may answer the question I came with. Reading the source can reveal a decision or uncertainty I had not considered. That is the return I want to understand: what makes a reader seek out the person behind an argument?</p>
<div class="pull-quote">
<p>An answer satisfies what you asked. Origin changes what you think to ask next. </p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block3 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 03</span> <h2 class="section-title">What Survives</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>The agentic internet is very good at moving information and very bad at preserving where the information came from. The agent that summarizes your piece extracts the claim and loses the standpoint. The reader gets the conclusion without the person who produced it.</p>
<p>What origin gravity describes is the behavior on the other side of that loss: the reader who goes around the filters, finds the source directly, subscribes not to the feed but to the person. Not common. Does not need to be common. In a web where agents handle most of the noise, the signal underneath becomes proportionally more valuable.</p>
<div class="margin-note">The empty room this series started with is real. The engagement metrics don't distinguish between a human and a process. But the room is not equally empty everywhere. Some creators still have people in it. Because origin gravity pulled those people through the noise.</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block4 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 04</span> <h2 class="section-title">What It Means to Create Now</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>Write from situations you can describe and questions you have actually worked through. Give the reader enough detail to examine how you reached the claim, including what changed your mind. Personal disclosure by itself does not provide that account.</p>
<p>A publishing schedule can distribute the work. It cannot supply the experience the account depends on. Describe what happened, what you chose, and what you still cannot explain.</p>

</div>
</div>
<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block5 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 05</span> <h2 class="section-title">The Answer</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>The first essay said: I think there's something in that relationship that doesn't survive delegation. But we've never had to name it, because we've never had to defend it.</p>
<p>The name is origin.</p>
<p>Here I focus on the individual writer. In <a href="occlusion-bias.html"><ArticleField slug="occlusion-bias" field="title" /></a>, Origin Gravity also concerns an editorial source whose choices and corrections readers can inspect. In either case, the question is whether an encounter with the work gives someone a reason to return to its source. It is a proposed relationship to test, not a property every human author automatically possesses.</p>
<p>Some readers will stop at the summary. Others may want to inspect the source and read more of its work. Origin Gravity is my name for that second possibility. The useful evidence would be people returning, subscribing, or responding over time.</p>
</div>
</div>
