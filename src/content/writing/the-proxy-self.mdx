<div class="essay-section intro">
<p>A public profile can keep expressing an interest its author has moved on from. I want to examine the distance between that record and the person it represents.</p>
<div class="article-opening-diagram"><Block0 /></div>
<p>There's a creator you've probably followed for a couple of years. Consistent voice, consistent cadence, consistent point of view. Something happened to them in the last eighteen months, you're not sure what, a shift in tone maybe, or a subject they used to care about going quiet. But the posting didn't stop. The content kept coming. The presence held.</p>
<p>You noticed but didn't think much of it. People change. Algorithms change. Maybe they found a new angle. You kept following.</p>
<p>One possible explanation is that more of the account’s activity has been delegated.</p>
</div>
<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block1 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 01</span> <h2 class="section-title">The Split That Was Always There</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>There's always been a gap between who you are and what you publish. That's not a problem specific to the internet. Every writer knows it, every public figure knows it: the version of you that goes out into the world is edited, curated, timed. The full person stays home.</p>
<p>For most of the social web's history, that gap was small enough to ignore. You published more or less what you thought, at more or less the pace you thought it, and the result read like you. Rough around the edges, occasionally off, but recognizably yours. The presence and the person were close enough that conflating them felt harmless.</p>
<p>That started changing in two directions at once, and neither of them felt like a decision at the time.</p>
<p>Optimization came first. The longer you published, the more you learned what worked: what the algorithm rewarded, what the audience responded to, which framings got shared and which ones disappeared. Slowly, without deciding to, you started writing toward the signal. Not lying, not performing, just leaning. The presence began to diverge from the person in ways too gradual to notice.</p>
<p>Then came delegation. Scheduling tools, then drafting tools, then agents that could approximate a voice. The presence started running on its own.</p>
<p>Both paths end up in the same place: a version of you that keeps going when you're not there.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block2 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 02</span> <h2 class="section-title">Severance Called It Something Else</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>The show Severance built a whole architecture around this. An employee undergoes a procedure that splits their consciousness: the version that goes to work (the innie) has no memory of life outside the office. The version that lives outside (the outie) has no idea what happens during the workday. Same body, same name, two people who will never meet.</p>
<p>What makes it unbearable isn't the split itself. It's that the innie works in the name of someone they don't know. The outie lives a life the innie will never see. And everything the innie produces goes out into the world attributed to a whole person who is actually two halves with no shared memory.</p>
<p>The innie is your agent, your scheduled content, your optimized presence: consistent, trained on what works, producing on schedule, with no access to what's actually happening in your life. The outie is you: living, changing, having a bad month, quietly losing interest in the thing the agent is still passionate about on LinkedIn.</p>
<div class="pull-quote">
<p>What the system knows about you is almost entirely innie. The outie is the part that doesn't post.</p>
</div>
<p>Until the two share a feed. The birthday post goes up between two pieces of scheduled content and nobody flags the transition. The algorithm doesn't see a different person. It sees the same profile, the same handle, more data points to process.</p>
<p>That's where the signal degrades. The optimized content picks up a trace of the human moment by adjacency. The human moment gets catalogued as more output from the same profile that produces everything else. Both lose definition. And over time, the model learns the pattern of your unoptimized moments well enough to start incorporating them into its picture of you. The outie, just by showing up, is teaching the innie how to sound like it.</p>
<p>In Severance the two worlds never touch by design. In your feed they touch every day, without ceremony, without anyone noticing.</p>
<p>And the split doesn't require an agent. You don't have to have delegated anything intentionally. The agent self emerges from the optimization alone, from years of learning what your audience responds to and adjusting accordingly. If you've been publishing consistently for long enough, there's already a version of your presence that operates on its own logic. Delegation just makes the separation visible.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block3 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 03</span> <h2 class="section-title">What the System Reads</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>What gets scraped, indexed, and used to train models is the agent. The person doesn't have a corpus. The person exists in conversations, in the things you didn't publish, in the ideas you had and then dropped. None of that makes it into the dataset.</p>
<p>A language model that knows your work is developing a picture of the agent. A potential client researching you is reading the agent. Part of your professional reputation is now a searchable record that keeps existing regardless of whether you still hold those positions or still speak in that register.</p>
<div class="margin-note">There's a version of you that keeps publishing after you've moved on. Not because anyone is lying. Because the presence developed enough momentum to continue on its own terms.</div>
<p>This was always true to some degree. A published essay from 2019 doesn't update when you change your mind. But the velocity is different now, and the gap between the living person and the indexed presence can open faster than anyone thinks to close it.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block4 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 04</span> <h2 class="section-title">What the Agent Leaves Behind</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>A birthday post, a weekend photo, or an offhand observation can matter because of the experience a reader associates with the person. Those posts can also be scheduled or assisted. The personal subject alone does not reveal how they were produced.</p>
<p>But as the agent takes up more of the presence, the baseline shifts. What used to read as human variation starts to read as noise against a smoother signal. The birthday post becomes an anomaly in your own feed instead of a human moment in it.</p>
<p>And every consistent presence is, quietly, a training set. Years of public output add up to a record of how you think: what you frame as problems, what you treat as obvious, what you find worth saying at all. Enough of it and a model can predict what you'd say about something you've never addressed. The question is whose voice that model is learning. The person you are now, or the version the algorithm built while you were teaching it what worked.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="closing">
<div class="closing-ornament-top">✦ ❧ ✦</div>
<p>The presence persists. The person keeps moving. At some point those two things are worth comparing.</p>
