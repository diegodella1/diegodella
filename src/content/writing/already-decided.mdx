<div class="content">

<div class="essay-section intro">
<p>Last night, at 3 AM, while I slept, a system running on a computer the size of a deck of cards in my house in Buenos Aires read 42 news articles, analyzed which ones affected the markets I care about, made three trading decisions, and executed two of them. When I woke up it had made money. Not much. The bot has a win rate hovering around 60 percent. It loses four out of ten. It wins and loses like almost everyone. The only one that always wins is the house.</p>
<div class="article-opening-diagram"><Block0 /></div>
<p>The decisions were already made before I opened my eyes.</p>
<p>Already decided.</p>

<p>I know which decisions I delegated because I programmed the systems. That habit of looking at decisions comes from metrics: conversion rates, acquisition costs, A/B tests. When I moved toward digital media, I kept encountering teams with more audience data than they could use. That gap between what we recorded and what we understood stayed with me.</p>
<p>In 2014, my co-founder and I entered that gap. I knew what was missing on the marketing side. He knew what the audience needed. Together we saw something the market did not see. Out of that came <a href="work-posta.html">Posta</a>, an early podcast production company in Argentina. By the 2022 PodX investment, public coverage reported more than 150 shows and branded work for more than 60 companies. PodX acquired a majority stake as its first investment in Latin America. The scale matters here because it came from joining two different ways of reading the same market, not because either of us could have built it alone.</p>
<p>I recognize that feeling again: a capability changes, and the organization keeps working around the old constraints. I am seeing it in the work I can now delegate. I want to understand which parts of the earlier media transition help explain it, and which do not.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<p>In media, I watched digital distribution change who could reach an audience and what an established organization needed to do. Buying a tool was only part of the adjustment. Teams also had to decide which work still justified their structure. That is the part of the comparison I find useful now.</p>
<p>In retail it happened the same way, five years later. E-commerce collapsed the cost per square meter. The stores that understood that the physical space was a point of relationship, not distribution, survived. The ones that kept optimizing SKUs per square meter are closing.</p>

<p>Hemingway wrote it better than any analyst could. Someone asks a character how he went bankrupt. He answers: two ways. Gradually, and then suddenly.</p>
<p>I recognize part of that feeling in the work I now delegate.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<p>The first bot I built was stupid. It did one thing. It did it well. And I had to be there for everything else. The day I gave it an objective instead of an instruction and it figured out the steps on its own, something changed. Not in the bot. In me. That is what an agent is: you do not tell it how, you tell it what. And it decides the how. It makes intermediate decisions you did not specify. It encounters problems you did not anticipate and works around them. I stopped thinking of it as a tool and started thinking of it as someone.</p>
<p>Then I started working with swarms. Groups of agents that coordinate with each other. They divide labor, share context, resolve conflicts on their own.</p>
<p>Right now, running from my house on small machines, I have a PR agency made of agents that manages multiple social media accounts autonomously. It publishes, responds, interacts with real people. Without my intervention. I have a software factory that takes a voice memo with an idea and turns it into a functional prototype ready for a demo in a couple of hours. I have the trading bot I told you about.</p>
<div class="pull-quote">
<p>And I have an advisory board. A swarm I use to discuss ideas, strategies, business decisions. It does not give me orders. It asks me questions. It challenges assumptions. It forces me to justify what I believe before I execute it. Last week I presented a restructuring idea to the board. It asked me three questions that my human team had not asked me in a month. Not because my team is bad. Because the swarm has no ego, no fear of confronting me, and no agenda of its own.</p>
</div>
<p>People who work with me say "Claude told me" the way they would say "the analyst told me" or "legal told me." It is a reference to a source of judgment.</p>
<p>Most companies that are adopting AI are in phase one. They are not doing anything wrong. They are doing what corresponds to the moment they are in. But it is phase one of a progression that has at least four. And most of them delegated the experimentation to someone three levels down the org chart. The understanding of the most consequential technology of their generation lives in someone they can replace but whose comprehension they cannot transfer.</p>
<p>Already decided. And they were not in the room.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<p>When a tool makes a task faster, the team has a choice about how to use the time. It can attempt more work, improve what it already produces, or change responsibilities. The decision needs evidence from the actual workflow.</p>
<p>When the work changes that fast, people also have to rethink the role they had built their identity around. A restructuring plan needs to make room for that change, alongside the new tasks and costs.</p>
<div class="margin-note">When you ask someone to go from executing to directing agents, you are asking them to change their relationship with their own work. Some thrive. Some get lost.</div>
<p>For the professional class, the company is where they spend most of their hours, where they derive most of their identity, where most of their social relationships operate. Think about what a company provides beyond a paycheck. A reason to leave the house. People who know your name. A role. Rituals. Shared complaints. All of it is social infrastructure. And it is invisible until it disappears.</p>
<p>I am not a theorist of this problem. I operate systems where AI amplifies what my team can produce. The output is better. The speed is different. The quality went up. But the transition is not free. The organization that does not understand that ends up with new tools and disoriented people.</p>
<p>There is someone reading this who depends on a platform to generate their leads. I do not know who. But I know that if tomorrow that platform changes its commissions, or its algorithm, or its rules, that person will discover that the relationship with their client was never theirs. It belonged to the platform.</p>
<p>The question is not whether it will happen. The question is whether, when it does, you will have a channel of your own that works. A database that is yours. A reputation that does not live on someone else's infrastructure.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<p>The question that matters is not what to automate. It is the inverse. What does your organization do that, if you automated it, you would lose the reason someone chooses you. That is what you protect. Everything else gets compressed.</p>
<p>Three questions that work as a permanent filter. For every decision about technology, structure, or vendor from here forward.</p>
<p>One. Does this decision bring me closer to or further from controlling the relationship with my client?</p>
<p>Two. If this vendor disappears tomorrow, how much of what I built on top of it survives?</p>
<p>Three. Is the team I am building sized for today's cost of iteration or for three years ago's?</p>
<p>If you cannot answer all three clearly, you have homework.</p>
</div>

<div class="closing">
<div class="closing-ornament-top">✦ ❧ ✦</div>
<p>I do not know how this ends. I can inspect the systems I built and the decisions they have already made, then ask whether I am still comfortable with the authority I gave them. That is where the next morning’s work starts.</p>

<p class="closing-final">Already decided.</p>
