<div class="essay-section intro">
<p>The numbers were fine. That was the first problem.</p>
<div class="article-opening-diagram"><Block0 /></div>
<p>Suppose you publish a piece and the engagement looks normal: visits, likes, perhaps a few replies. You still need to know what those events represent. How many people read the original, and how much activity came from automated systems?</p>
<p>You probably filed it under off week. Algorithm change. Audience fatigue. There's always an explanation available when you need one.</p>
<p>But the feeling came back. And then again.</p>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block1 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 01</span> <h2 class="section-title">The Instrument</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>Engagement metrics were always measuring behavior. Not presence, not attention, not a human on the other side of the screen who actually read the thing. Behavior. The click. The scroll-stop. The repost. A signal that something happened, with no information about who made it happen.</p>
<p>For twenty years that didn't matter much, because behavior mostly implied a human. We knew it wasn't perfect: click fraud existed, click farms existed, whole industries grew up around detecting and filtering the noise. But the assumption held well enough to build on. Non-human behavior was the exception you corrected for. The signal, underneath the fraud, still pointed to a person.</p>
<p>Automation is not one category. Search crawlers, monitoring tools, malicious bots, and assistants acting for users do different work. <a href="https://www.imperva.com/resources/wp-content/uploads/sites/6/reports/2025-Bad-Bot-Report.pdf">Imperva’s 2025 report</a> describes a broad automated-traffic category, not a count of delegated readers. For your publication, check which requests the analytics record and how they are separated from human visits.</p>
<div class="pull-quote">
<p>The room didn't empty overnight. It filled, with something that behaves exactly like the audience used to behave.</p>
</div>
<p>Agents are browsing. Agents are transacting. The web already has llms.txt, a proposed standard modeled on robots.txt, that tells AI crawlers how to read your site. When the infrastructure starts building protocols specifically for non-human visitors, that's not a warning sign. That's a receipt. It already happened, while you were looking at your analytics and feeling vaguely unsettled.</p>
<p>The dashboard still glows. The echo is just slightly wrong.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block2 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 02</span> <h2 class="section-title">What It Proves</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>The problem isn't that the audience changed. Audiences change all the time, that's the whole history of media. The problem is that the instrument we use to measure the audience was never measuring what we thought. It was measuring behavior. And behavior doesn't require a person anymore.</p>
<p>So what does any of it prove? A high-engagement post proves the algorithm rewarded it. A high-traffic article proves something requested it. Neither tells you whether a human read the thing, cared about it, or will think about it tomorrow.</p>
<div class="margin-note">The content is still being produced. The metrics are still being generated. The feedback loop is still spinning. It's just that somewhere in that loop, the human became optional.</div>
<p>The dashboard still glows the same color it always did.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block3 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 03</span> <h2 class="section-title">The Real Cost</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>This isn't an argument against publishing, and it's not a warning about what's coming. The thing that bothers me is simpler: the metrics we use to evaluate whether we're reaching people were never designed to distinguish between a person and a process that mimics one.</p>
<p>They still aren't.</p>
<p>Which means every decision downstream of those metrics (what to publish, when, for whom, in what register) is being made on the basis of a signal that has always been a proxy, and is now a proxy for something increasingly different from what we intended. The optimization loop is intact. It's just that nobody checked what it's optimizing toward.</p>
<p>Think about what that means in practice. You A/B test a headline. The variant with more clicks wins. You write more headlines like it. The system learns. You learn. Both of you get better at producing something that generates behavior. But if the behavior is increasingly generated by agents reading for indexing, summarizing for retrieval, or scanning for relevance signals on behalf of a user who will never see your page at all, then you've spent six months getting very good at writing for a reader who isn't there. The reader who isn't there still generates behavior.</p>
<p>Traffic can continue to rise while fewer people read the work directly.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="essay-section">
<div class="section-badge">
<div class="section-icon"><Block4 /></div>
<div class="section-meta"> <span class="section-number">Section 04</span> <h2 class="section-title">The Question This Reading Path Will Carry</h2> </div>
</div>
<div class="section-rule"></div>
<div class="section-body">
<p>That leaves a question for the rest of this series: what makes a reader want a relationship with a particular source?</p>
<p>If engagement measures behavior, and agents can produce behavior, is there anything in the relationship between a creator and an audience that an agent fundamentally cannot simulate?</p>

<p>I think yes. I think there's something in that relationship that doesn't survive delegation. But we've never had to name it, because we've never had to defend it. We just called it "connection" and assumed the metrics were picking it up.</p>
<p>They weren't. They were tracking clicks.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="closing-ornament-top">✦ ❧ ✦</div>
<p>The clicks are still coming. The question is who's making them.</p>
